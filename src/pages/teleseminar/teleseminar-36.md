### Special subject: Medicare CGM coverage and meter accuracy ratings

There's been a new regulation or an unregulated submitted by the FDA in the, I'm sorry, submitted by
Medicare in the USA. So recently, Medicare banned the use of continuous glucose monitors that had a
follower option where another person could see your blood sugars and send an emergency unit over if
you did not reply to their warnings to take some glucose to treat a low blood sugar or even to deal
with high blood sugars.

I predicted on one of my videos some months ago that sooner or later, some congressmen with diabetes
in their family would get together and set things straight. In fact, I said it would probably take a
vote of congress.

Well, what actually did happen and has been reported is that several congressmen complained to the
director of Medicare about this regulation which had no logical scientific or health reason for it
and probably was born of connections with companies that did not provide follower systems.

The whole thing has been reversed and within a matter of weeks to a couple of months, Medicare will
be paying for systems that allow followers. So I will no longer have to pay for my Dexcom system
which I've been paying for for years.

The other point that I'd like to talk about has to do with studies of the accuracy of blood sugar
meters and of continuous glucose monitors even those associated with insulin pumps. One has to be
leery of the ratings because frequently the individuals who do the studies or write the ratings are
associated with manufacturers of these pieces of equipment. So there may be a set up in the ratings.

Another thing and there have been several, there was recently a published rating of blood sugar
meters and there was also a published rating of continuous glucose monitors. One thing I noticed in
the reports both of the meters and the monitors is that the data was not broken down by blood sugar
level so that the reports don't tell you this meter is the most accurate at blood sugars over 200,
this is the most accurate at blood sugars in the normal range, etc. etc.

And since we're seeking blood sugars in the normal range, we don't care if a meter is inaccurate at
blood sugars of 300 or 400 or even 200. So unless a rating system gives you a breakdown of where
these meters are accurate or inaccurate, we have no information as to whether they'll be useful for
us because we're looking for the normal range.

So you have to be careful when you look at these ratings. You also have to be careful when you look
at ratings that evaluate much more than accuracy. For example, several years ago, Consumer Reports,
a magazine in the USA, reviewed blood sugar meters and rated them principally upon the number of
special features that they had rather than upon their accuracy.

So just a warning, be careful, don't necessarily believe what you see in these reports.

### My husband has been implementing your protocol for about 4 months now with good results. However, he is puzzled by blood glucose spikes he gets after exercising at the gym. He does mostly resistance workouts with some cardio. How would he go about preventing the big spike? [short: Prevent post-exercise glucose spikes?]

Well, I've seen this in some of my patients, not all of them, and I concluded or I made a guess that
the activity increases the production of stress hormones, epinephrine, cortisol, glucose hormone,
and that the activity was not strenuous enough to offset the glucose increase caused by the stress
hormones.

So I asked these people to take a small dose of propranolol, which is a beta blocker that
inhibits the production of epinephrine, which is probably the major stress hormone that you make
when you exercise, and I wasn't surprised, but I was encouraged by the fact that indeed the small
dose of propranolol, about 10 milligrams, taken an hour to an hour and a half before the exercise
tends to prevent this spike.

I can't guarantee that it will prevent it for your husband, but there's a good chance that it may.
So your doctor can prescribe it, and you have to experiment a little with the timing. For some
people, taking it a half hour before works, so I'd experiment with a half hour, an hour, and an hour
and a half. I don't think you have to go any longer than an hour and a half in advance, and a half
hour might work.

### Since you don't recommend insulin pens, how do you inject Tresiba? Do you extract it with a regular insulin syringe? [short: How do you inject Tresiba without a pen?]

Well, I don't recall not recommending insulin pens. In fact, I don't think anywhere in the book do I
speak against insulin pens. I do point out that there's a big problem with leakage from the
injection site in people who use insulin pens, and there's another problem in that insulin pens
cannot be set for injecting half units and quarter units. So for little children, they may be
inappropriate. So they don't contain diluted insulin as children may require.

I do use Tresiba, and I use a pen, and maybe I'm doing something that's a little bit inappropriate,
but I first, when I give an injection, I hold the button down for about 40 seconds. Then I release
the button, leaving the needle still in my skin for another 40 seconds, and usually I get little if
no leakage. I rub my hand on the skin after the injection to see if it's wet, and if I feel that
it's wet, I'll inject one more unit to replace what was lost.

That's not as precise as I would tell my patients to do, and I do have several patients who are
using an ordinary insulin syringe to extract the Tresiba from the Tresiba cartridges. They don't
even put the cartridges in the pen. They hold them up to the syringe and stick the needle in the
cartridge. It's pretty obvious how you can do this.

The one catch is you must pull back the plunger on the syringe very slowly because as you're
withdrawing Tresiba insulin, the gasket at the other end of the cartridge is moving, and there's a
lot of friction there. If you pull rapidly, the gasket won't move, and you'll have vacuum in your
syringe, and that vacuum will draw some bubbles up from the back of the syringe from the far end of
the plunger.
