Welcome to another session of Dr. Bernstein's Diabetes University. Today we're going to talk
about basal bolus insulin dosing, but because it's a lengthy subject we're going to start with
just the basal part.

Basal bolus means an attempt to imitate the way non-diabetic humans make
insulin. Dogs and cats and other animals also make insulin the same way. They make insulin
continually around the clock even if they're not eating a small amount that stops the body
from breaking down its own proteins, muscles and so on to maintain blood sugar. And the bolus,
the insulin that people make is at meal time or whenever they're eating something that could raise
blood sugar. 

In reality anything you eat can raise blood sugar so humans make insulin,
extra insulin when they are eating things and when the things they eat are being digested. So
we're talking only now about basal and nowadays there are to my knowledge only three basal
insulins being made. By the way the best basal insulin was discontinued for marketing reasons,
not for rational reasons, not for the patient's benefit and that was Ultralente insulin.

We now
have Tresiba, chemical name Degludec, as a long-acting or basal insulin and we have Glargine
also called Lantus. I'll talk about the brand names not the generic names because they're
more comfortable for me. Now both Tresiba and Lantus have problems of storage. Once the bottle,
the vial is punctured, the insulin maintains its strength for about 40 days for the Lantus
and 56 days for the Tresiba if kept in the refrigerator. If kept out of the refrigerator
they will probably not last that long. So people are wise to keep these particular brands of
insulin in the refrigerator and if you're traveling you should at the very least use a
bag of Frio.

We've illustrated it on a prior session. You put the inner bag in a
sink of cold water for 20 minutes no longer and then you can put the insulin vial if you're
traveling inside the bag and put the inner bag in an outer bag and you could carry it with you
when you're traveling. If you're at home use the refrigerator. If you're staying in a hotel after
traveling keep the insulin in the refrigerator. The Frio will lower the temperature of the insulin
by about 10 or 12 degrees but you should keep it in a refrigerator when possible. This is both the
Tresiba and Lantus. The Tresiba lasts a little longer than the Lantus. They claim 47 days. I
tell people which to use only Tresiba for reasons I'll go into and I tell them to discard the vial
after 30 days.

These insulins are also available in pens and depending upon the pen you use you
may or may not be able to get fine adjustment of doses. To me fine adjustment means able to
adjust the dose by one half unit increments. Lantus unfortunately has 165 times the affinity
for GLP-1 receptors than the Tresiba. GLP-1 receptors are growth hormone receptors that
appear on cancer cells and are stimulated by insulin. So if you have any cancer even one that
is minuscule and about to be destroyed by your immune system the Lantus will stimulate these
cancers to proliferate. This has been challenged by the manufacturers and supposedly proven not
to be true but the proof is in very short-term studies where cancers require 10, 20 or more
years to develop.

So Lantus has not been shown to be a safe insulin. The Tresiba does not have
this problem. Neither of these insulins last 24 hours as claimed by their manufacturers. Although
some type 2 diabetics who are making a lot of their own insulin may get away with one shot per
day of Lantus or Tresiba. The type 1s cannot. 

So what the endocrinologists and manufacturers
are advising is don't take a basal dose. Take a super physiologic dose or an industrial dose of
these insulins in the hope that it'll last for 24 hours. But it'll also force you to be eating for
24 hours because a basal insulin does not cover food. But if you take so much of it that it'll
last a long time you have to eat food to cover the excessive amount of insulin. 

So what we
recommend is that you use only basal amounts and amount to keep you level when you're fasting and
that you take it twice a day. And the twice a day is not every 12 hours as many endocrinologists
recommend. It is on a rising in the morning and at bedtime. Not only that, the bedtime dose is
not likely to last more than eight and a half hours or so because of something called the dawn
phenomenon.

So if you're dealing with a small child who's going to sleep 10 hours you're going
to have to give him his morning shot while he's sleeping. If giving it in his butt with a short
needle doesn't wake him up that would be great. But you don't give these things too far apart
overnight. I would say no more than eight and a half hours. This is against the claims of the
manufacturers, against common practice, probably against the recommendations of the professional
societies, but this is what works.

And I know because I've been treating my own diabetes for
70 years and I treat many diabetics and I'm able to give them essentially normal blood sugars,
not by following the rules but by doing what works.
