Hi, this is Dr. Bernstein with another issue of Dr. Bernstein's Diabetes University.
This time we're going to talk about foot care.
It's a subject that's a lot more important than one might think.
The amputation rate in diabetics runs somewhere between around 10%.
The lifespan of a diabetic after an amputation of a foot or a leg is about 5 years on average.
So it adversely affects lifespan.

Now amputations don't have to happen.
There are changes in the shape of the foot and in other aspects of the foot that we'll
talk about due to high blood sugars.
If you have normal blood sugars you don't have to worry about these changes.
So the first thing you do if you're worried about your feet is make sure you have normal
blood sugars.

If you have elevated blood sugars, even slightly elevated, a number of changes will occur.
One change is peripheral sensory neuropathy where sensation is impaired and you're less
likely to feel injuries, burns, punctures, etc.
So you're more likely to get hurt if you can't feel anything.

Another problem is what's called sympathetic neuropathy where things that are stimulated
by the sympathetic nervous system like perspiration ceases or gets diminished.
Well most long term diabetics who I meet for the first time have extremely dry feet
because they cannot sweat and dry skin is more likely to get injured and much slower
to heal.

So if you catch it early and have normal blood sugars from the onset of diabetes you won't
get dry skin.
But if you do get dry skin you should lubricate it.
And what I recommend is lubricating with any animal or vegetable oil and I've treated thousands
of patients in our wound care clinic and I find olive oil to be extremely effective.
So olive oil is fine.
I don't like lotions, they don't seem to get absorbed as well as the oils.
So olive oil is fine.
We don't want thick oils.
I used to recommend mink oil but it's too thick and sticky, it's harder to use.
Olive oil is more fluid.
Coconut oil might be okay also.
That's lubrication.

The most common, one other thing I should mention is that the circulation to the feet
gets impaired if you have long standing elevated blood sugars.
And when you have poor circulation, wounds are less likely to heal, they're more likely
to get infected because the immune system that gets transported via the bloodstream
to the wound isn't getting there adequately if you have poor circulation.

Furthermore, nutrients that are needed for healing and even oxygen doesn't get to the
wound if you have poor circulation.
Now the most common cause of amputations and almost the only cause is attempts to remove
calluses.
It's interesting that for years the American Diabetes Association recommended that calluses
be removed by a trained professional with a scalpel.

And then I published in one of their journals the results of my observations over 30 years
of serving in the wound care clinic.
And I interviewed every diabetic patient who attended our clinic and of course every diabetic
patient who had had an amputation, whether it was a toe, a foot, or a lower leg, or the
entire leg above the knee.

And in every case I asked, what led to the amputation?
Every person said to me, someone tried to remove a callus.
The most common was a podiatrist.
Next most common was the patient or a family member.
There were also manicurists who were giving foot treatments.
So and the modes of removal were varied.
It could have been a pumice stone by a podiatrist.
It could have been a surgeon using a scalpel.
It could have been the patient using a little battery operated callus grinder that the ADA
advertises in their patient publications.
All of these different modes were the cause of the amputation.

Now there may be rare situations where it's necessary to remove a callus.
In the 30 years or so that I spent in the wound care clinic, I encountered one man,
one person, it was a man, it was a homeless man who had very thick calluses about two
inches thick.
And they were pressing on his shoes, how he got his feet into his shoes, I don't know.
And I cut them down until they were maybe a quarter of an inch thick.
I was very careful not to cut too close, not to remove them totally because I didn't want
to create a wound.

Now some people may not like calluses because for aesthetic reasons.
A thin callus has no medical reason for removal.
Some surgeons say well there could be an ulcer under it.
That's speculation and when you see extremely poor foot care, very tight shoes, maybe there
might be an ulcer under the callus but you can tell by just feeling the skin for heat
whether there's an infection going on.

You don't have to cut off the callus.
If you do think that there's likelihood of an ulcer, then you have to be extremely careful.
Now there are ways of getting rid of calluses if you don't like the looks of them because
you're temperamental on what your feet look like.
And that is to either widen the shoe or to do something to get rid of the pressure on
the point where the callus is.

Frequently that means putting orthotics into your shoes to take the pressure off the callus
and put it on the arch of the foot, move the pressure to the arch of the foot.
This requires customized orthotics to be inserted in your shoes.
The shoe has to be bigger to fit the orthotics.
You may have to buy a new shoe to fit the orthotic.

But the important thing is that calluses are not to be removed.
It's asking for trouble.
I actually watched in a surgical clinic when a young lady about 30 years old had intrinsic
minus feet.
These are the deformed feet that diabetics get from high blood sugars.
And her metatarsal heads, the bumps on the bottom of her feet where the bones end, the
bony heads, were protruding because of her foot deformity.
And she had maybe calluses that were a sixteenth of an inch thick, maybe an eighth of an inch
thick.

And there was a physician's assistant with a scalpel slicing off these calluses till
each metatarsal head was bleeding, wrapped the whole foot with gauze, sent her home without
any arch support or anything to take the pressure off those metatarsal heads.
So here she was going to go home with pressure on the metatarsal heads that were now open
and bleeding and the pressure stops the healing.
So she's guaranteed to come back with infected wounds in the bottoms of both feet and probably
will have both legs amputated.

I was at someone else's clinic.
I could not butt in, but it was torture to watch what was going on.
Now I published my observations in one of the diabetes journals and the following year
and I recommended at the end that the ADA remove from their guidelines the requirement
that calluses be cut off.
And indeed the next year the guidelines no longer required that.
But now in 2015 they have new guidelines.
They forgot the old article and they're again requiring that calluses be cut off surgically.

Another important thing that you should remember is that your shoes must fit.
Don't wear pointed toe shoes.
Wear shoes with a high toe box so there's room for your toes especially if you have
hammer toes like this which are part of the deformity that diabetics can get from high
blood sugars.
So you want a deep toe box.
You want a wide toe box and never pointed toe shoes.
And if you get any pink spots you're going to have to do something to modify your shoes
so that there's no pressure because pressure will cause initially pink spots and eventually
calluses.
That's about all I can tell you right now.
Good luck.
