This time we're going to examine the question of why people in authority are
distorting the results of scientific studies to make it seem that normal blood
sugars are to be avoided in diabetes. In particular we're going to talk this time
about the <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0002914907004377" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"> ACCORD trial </a>. 

Let's see if we could figure out what that
stands for. Action to Control Cardiovascular Risk in Diabetes. Now this
was a study that was reported in the 88 journals in 2010.

As published in the journal it came to the conclusion that when they tried to improve blood sugars, not to
normalize them, but get A1Cs down to 6 or below, under 7 certainly, that they
were increasing mortality.

It's interesting that I ran into one of the
doctors who was cited as an author to the study and she told me that the group
of them, there were a number of them, were called into a room, told what the
conclusions of the study were going to be and had some discussion and no change
was made based upon the input of the practitioners in the study, the doctors
who participated in the study, even though they disagreed with the
conclusions.

So the conclusions were frozen in without their approval and
came to the conclusion that lowering blood sugar does not help cardiac
disease. And as it was published, well, a press release then came out by the, made
by the American Diabetes Association, saying that improving blood sugar may
help eyes, nerves, and kidneys, but it certainly doesn't help the heart. 

By the way, the study was only about three and a half years, so it's not a long-term study
that would usually be necessary if you wanted to prevent cardiovascular
disease. Anyway, one of the participants in the study came up with an analysis of
the data that was quite different from the ADA's analysis and if you read this
study carefully you'll see that his analysis was far more correct and this
is what he says, published in the same issue of the journal Diabetes Care, but
at the end of the issue, where you'll only get to it if you're exhausted from
reading everything else, if you're not exhausted from reading everything else,
overall they found no evidence to suggest that lower average A1c was
associated with higher mortality as would have been expected if hypoglycemia
had been the cause of death.

Now the ADA conclusion, of course, was just the
opposite of what the data said. In fact, a higher mortality rate was observed in
both the intensive and standard therapy arms for individuals with a higher
average and average and last recorded A1c.

In other words, if the last A1c in
the study was high or if the average A1c was high, those are the patients who
died. 

The patients with A1cs at the low end were the ones who survived.

So the study, even though it was something like three and a half years long, was not very
long, did demonstrate that lowering the A1c provides cardiac benefits. Now this
accord study, because of the erroneous press release by the ADA, and that's what
hit the newspapers, we have endocrinologists, regular doctors, all
kinds of people concluding that low A1cs are bad for the heart and are
to be avoided.

Now why do you think erroneous conclusions were drawn? Are
there any benefits to anybody by drawing erroneous conclusions? I can think of two.

I can't say that the erroneous conclusions were deliberately made, but I
can say that they benefit two classes of individuals. One class would be companies
that make or sell medications for treating the complications of
diabetes, especially for treating heart disease.

If you will advocate higher
blood sugars cause more heart disease, which is what the study showed, you're
going to sell more drugs for hypertension, drugs for treating
coagulation. Probably dozens of different drugs will have improved sales by making
the hearts worse in diabetics.

So if you go for high blood sugars, cause heart
disease, you'll generate more business. But you'll also generate millions of
dollars in advertising for these drugs for publications earmarked for diabetes,
such as the publications of the ADA.

There's another class of individuals who
benefit from high blood sugars in diabetics. Those who do not know how to
control blood sugar and who continue to advocate high
carbohydrate diets and industrial doses of medications like insulin.

These people
put themselves at great risk for someone dying of hypoglycemia because they're
giving supraphysiologic doses of insulin. And as I've been told by a
number of big shots in the field, look, I treat thousands of diabetics. If they
were all to go blind or die of congestive heart failure or have their
legs amputated, those are natural consequences of the disease.

And such a
person will say to me, blood sugars have nothing to do with it. But if one person
out of say 2,000 or 3,000 dies of hypoglycemia, I get sued. Not me, but the
guy who's talking. So there are doctors who are not treating diabetes in a
knowledgeable way, who are at risk for getting sued for causing hypoglycemic
death. If from high carbohydrate diets or the industrial doses of insulin they
use, those people are at risk too.

So by drawing the wrong conclusion from a
study, you can make their lives easier, justify their giving people very high
blood sugars, and of course if you have a blood sugar that's three times normal,
you have much lower risk of hypoglycemia.

Well, that's not quite true
because studies have been done showing that there are as many major
hypoglycemic episodes for people with high A1Cs as with low A1Cs. So it's a
myth that you're going to be doing them any benefit.

But all in all, this study
being repeatedly misquoted is hurting people and probably killing people. I
just had a medical education tape today by a noted authority supposedly giving
me monthly training so I could maintain all my licenses, and he's telling me that
blood sugar control is of no value to the heart, only the eyes and the kidneys
and the nerves. So that's basically the story.
