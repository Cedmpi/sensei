### Special subject: DPP-4 inhibitors and pancreatitis risk

Before we get started, we have a couple of special subjects that came up in a recent issue of
[Diabetes Care published by the American Diabetes Association](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article-abstract/42/11/2057/36346/Nationwide-Trends-in-Pancreatitis-and-Pancreatic). One dealt with a class of drugs called
DPP-4 antagonists. They're also called glyptins.

Some of the names of the drugs are Sitagliptin,
Saxagliptin, Vildagliptin, and I've deliberately not used this class of drugs for any of my patients
because when I first started to use them, I found that they had almost no effect on blood sugar,
very small effect on blood sugar, and did not curb appetite or overeating as the GLP-1 agonists did,
so there was no purpose in them.

However, they're widely used and they work by destroying the enzyme that destroys GLP-1. The body
naturally makes GLP-1 while eating, and GLP-1 has many benefits, including increasing satiety,
making you satisfied with your meal. However, the body also makes an enzyme, dipeptidase number 4,
DPP-4, that destroys GLP-1, and the glyptins destroy the destroyer.

Anyway, it's now reported that there's a 24% increase in pancreatitis in the people who take the
GLP-1s and an 81% increase in pancreatic cancer. The cancer is pretty rare, but nevertheless, this
increase exists after only six months on a glyptin.

They also found that by waiting longer, maybe a year or two, the percentage of increase did not
increase, so there are hazards. It's of a low level on the cancer, but I found that GLP-1 agonists
actually caused pancreatitis in several of my patients, and I may have only had, let's say, 50
patients on the GLP-1s. That would have meant something like 6% of my patients were getting
pancreatitis, so I think the incidence of pancreatitis from both classes of drugs, the DPP-4s
antagonists and the GLP-1 agonists is much more than has been reported.

### Special subject: Edmonton Protocol and immunosuppression

Another [article in the journal Diabetes Care](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article-abstract/42/11/2042/36368/Ten-Year-Outcome-of-Islet-Alone-or-Islet-After)
recently gave a 10 year update on the Edmonton
Protocol. This is a protocol for supposedly curing diabetes that is performed at the University of
Alberta in Edmonton, Canada. They transplant beta cells from the pancreases of cadavers to humans,
human diabetics, and the beta cells remain functioning due to immunosuppression of the recipients.

In my experience, immunosuppression is worse than diabetes, much worse. It exposes you to infections
that are not fought off by the immune system and also to malignancies, a wide range of malignancies.

Now, why do I feel strongly about this? Because I've had an immunosuppressing disease, CVID, since
childhood, probably since I was born, and I already have developed throughout my life many severe
infections.

I had severe disabling sinusitis, C. difficile infection of the intestines, which often is fatal.
I've had treatments for the immunosuppression that caused glaucoma and rendered me almost blind.

I have a long list of problems associated with immunosuppression. I don't have any long-term
problems associated with my diabetes because in 1970 or thereabouts, I managed to normalize my blood
sugars. It's much more efficacious to learn to normalize your blood sugars than it is to endure
immunosuppression.

These patients were put on very potent immunosuppressants. The long article on the update gave all
kinds of details and very briefly mentioned some of the outcomes including, I believe, several
cancers, a number of infections, et cetera. There were several amputations and other elements that
could be associated with diabetes.

The question, of course, arises, was it due to the diabetes that preceded the islet cell transplants
or was it because the blood sugars after the islet cell transplants were not that great?

Their target A1C was, I believe 6.5%, which would have been an average blood sugar
of 160 mg/dL, which is about double normal.

What they actually achieved was a range of A1C values from 6.1%, which would be about 144 mg/dL average,
to 8.0%, which would be an average of 220 mg/dL.

These people throughout the study did not have normal blood sugars and could have developed diabetic
complications.

It could be that the increase in kidney disease and amputations during the study were brought about
by the higher blood sugars during the study or they could have been brought about by the higher
blood sugars that preceded the study.

But all in all, it seems to me that it's much wiser to teach patients how to normalize their blood
sugars than it is to put them on potent immunosuppressants.
