### Special subject: Ketones and protein needs in children on low-carb diets

Today we're going to have a special subject because I've had many questions both by email and for
our Teleseminar from parents of small children who are complaining that since they put them on a low
carbohydrate diet, they're showing ketones in their urine.

Well the first point is that ketones are not harmful but they are a sign of something. What's
harmful is high blood sugars but kids don't have much of a blood sugar reserve in terms of glycogen
stores. They can use up their stored glycogen very rapidly. So if they're on a low carbohydrate diet
and they're physically active, they're going to use up a lot of stored glycogen which becomes
glucose as they need it and they need a backup supply.

The backup supply is protein and in the case of patients that have actually seen in every one of
these cases, the parents have been giving the kid inadequate amounts of protein. In fact, the kids
usually have not been growing when they come here for the first visit. We normalize their blood
sugars and we give them much more protein than they had been getting before. Maybe triple,
quadruple, five times as much as the parents had been giving them.

There are these myths on the internet that you should give people a lot of fat and I maintain that
whatever fat comes with the protein, that's the fat that you get. What you need is the protein.

You do need some essential fatty acids but they come with the protein foods so I cannot overemphasize
the need for protein no matter what sort of diet you're on if you're a child but certainly if you're
on a low carbohydrate diet, you're going to need it as a source of glucose.

In fact, I think the very earliest paper that I got published was long before I became a physician
was an abstract of a talk that I gave at a medical conference, a diabetes conference and it was
published in Acta Paediatrica Belgica, a Belgian publication and the title was Protein as the
principle source of glucose in the treatment of diabetes and that sort of sums it up.

Gluconeogenesis is a chemical process by which the body converts protein to glucose as needed so
instead of loading the patient with carbohydrate that rapidly raises blood sugar and cannot be
controlled by injected insulin because the rise is so great, you give protein and it gets converted
to glucose as needed and you could cover it with small doses of insulin.
