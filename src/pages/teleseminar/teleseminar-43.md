### Special subject: Blood sugar test strips for type 2 diabetes

We have a special subject today relating to an article in a recent issue of the New York Times. The
title of the article is, Diabetes Care Can Be Costly Yet Wasteful.

The author of this article claims that for type 2 diabetes, measurement of blood sugar, which
Medicare will pay for 3 strips a day, is a waste of money and may be harmful in some way.

The reason they have this article is because there was a report in one of the diabetes journals to
the effect that for type 2 diabetics, number one, measuring blood sugar doesn't seem to make a
difference in their hemoglobin A1C, and number two, even though Medicare may pay them to buy strips,
most of the type 2 diabetics don't use the strips anyway.

In fact, many type 2 diabetics are actually selling the strips that Medicare pays for, selling them
to type 1 diabetics or to brokers who in turn sell the strips to type 1 diabetics. If that's what's
happening, then the strips are being wasted. You can't say giving them the type 1 diabetics is a
waste, but the Medicare money is not going to accomplish anything for the Medicare recipients.

However, the big problem with type 2 diabetics and blood sugar test strips is that most physicians
do not know how to utilize the information from the blood sugar tests.

Furthermore, 3 strips a day for a type 2 diabetic are not adequate at the time that you start to
treat them. If I'm treating a type 2 diabetic, I need something of the order of 10 blood sugars a
day before I can work out their diet, their medications, and so on. By the end, and so on, I mean
not just what medications, but the amounts of the medications and the timing of the medications.
Timing can be very important.

So once I've started a new type 2 patient on a new regimen, new diet, I have to see I might be
looking first at just the effects of diet, so I need blood sugars before and for 1 or 2 hours
after each meal. If I'm giving medication, I may need blood sugars during the night. I need, as I
said, at least 10 blood sugars a day in a new type 2 patient.

Eventually, if I get them stabilized on the right diet and the right meds and they adhere to this, I
may only need 1 day's worth of blood sugar a week. I might need for that day only 5 blood
sugars or 6 blood sugars, but to blanketly say that three blood sugars a day as paid for by
Medicare is a waste of money and is potentially harmful, is correct for most diabetics, but not for
the diabetics who are trying to keep their blood sugars normal and are using my book as a guide.

They are going to need a lot of strips every day while they're working out their initial regimen.

Now we'll get down to the questions.

### Can you explain LADA, that's latent autoimmune diabetes, and what type of diet one should follow to gain weight? I use small doses of basal insulin and increased my carbs to 45 grams per meal due to weight loss. [short: What diet helps someone with LADA gain weight?]

First of all, I've explained LADA in the past, and LADA is a disease that has variable effects
depending on the individual and the genetics of the individual.

People with LADA frequently require insulin injections. Sometimes they're insulin resistant and
require large doses. Sometimes they need very small doses.

So there are various types of LADA, and the type of LADA you have has nothing to do with the diet.
The diet should be as low in carb as possible, just as described in my book Diabetes Solution.

To gain weight, you don't increase the carbs and increase the insulin. If you want to gain weight,
you increase the protein and increase the insulin. Increasing the carbs increases the variability of
the results. Carbs have a much greater effect on blood sugar than does protein. So we recommend
increasing protein and increasing insulin in order to gain weight.

### What do you think about the closed-loop products out there today? Not so much for the mealtime boluses since those are minimal with Dr. Bernstein's diet but for the basal rate. [short: Closed-loop devices]

The closed-loop devices are devices that are measuring your blood sugar and telling an insulin pump
how much insulin to give to you.

This is a very reasonable question because type 1 diabetics have to worry about the dawn phenomenon
where for a few hours in the early morning, you need more insulin. The pump, in theory, could take
care of that for you.

You could have a constant basal rate but for a few hours before you get up in the morning and after
you're up in the morning, increase the basal rate.

That in theory works but in practice, there are problems. You have a foreign body under the skin
injecting the insulin that's there all the time causing inflammation initially and that inflammation
can lead to unpredictable absorption of insulin but eventually, you get scar tissue at these
infusion sites and the scar tissue inhibits the absorption and makes it even more unpredictable.

On top of that, when you're talking closed-loop, you have a system that is supposedly measuring your
blood sugar and dosing out insulin in response to your blood sugar.

The trouble is that the sensor is not really measuring blood sugar. It's measuring interstitial
glucose levels, the glucose level in tissue fluid between the cells in the subcutaneous tissue and
that tends to run about a half an hour behind blood sugar.

Right away, your data is out of phase to the extent of a half an hour and the algorithms that are
fed into these devices for adjusting to blood sugar are not necessarily going to work the same for
each individual in part because the absorption of insulin is going to be different for each
individual depending upon the infusion site and so on. I would not recommend these devices for this
purpose.

What we do to circumvent the dawn phenomenon is use the longest-acting insulin we can find. We give
it on a rising and at bedtime.

For some people, full type 1s who are not making any insulin, they may have to get their second
basal dose instead of on a rising in the morning several hours before they arise. We actually do
have people waking up a few hours early in order to get a shot. With kids, the parent can do it.

Another thing that we may be doing is giving a rapid-acting insulin on a rising to prevent a blood
sugar increase after you get up. I just today spoke to parents of 2 children who are getting
diluted Humalog insulin on a rising to prevent a blood sugar rise.

### What is the best resistant starch that passes through to the large intestines without being digested or raising blood sugar? How does resistant starch affect blood sugar? [short: How does resistant starch affect blood sugar?]

To my knowledge, there's no such thing as a starch that does not raise blood sugar. There are
starches that raise blood sugar more or less than others.

For example, the two most common starches are amylose and amylopectin. Amylose is straight chains of
glucose molecules. These chains can, in a product, be lying flat one on top of another so that the
digestive enzymes that would ordinarily rapidly convert the amylose to glucose have trouble getting
in between the chains.

Amylopectin has branches, so it's like a tree with branches sticking out. These branches prevent the
underlying chains from occluding one another, so you get empty spaces between the branches, which
allows the enzyme to creep in to more rapidly digest the product.

The difference is one of speed. The amylose will be digested slowly. The amylopectin will be
digested more rapidly.

If any company claims to have a resistant starch that will not affect your blood sugar, they're
probably being deceptive. These starches will have some effect.

### If a youngster 10 years of age is newly diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, would it ever be possible to allow the beta cells to heal and the need for insulin to vanish? [short: Beta cell recovery in newly diagnosed?]

Well, not right now.

Down the road, there are projects going on looking for ways to stimulate stem cells for new beta
cells to replicate.

There are several known stem cells for beta cells in the body. These include the alpha cells of the
pancreas that make glucagon. They can also transform to beta cells that make insulin.

They're the endothelial cells that line the ducts of the pancreas through the ducts through which

Down the road, this may take place, but in the meanwhile, you should be giving your child normal
blood sugars, which is much easier to achieve when they're first diagnosed because they're likely to
be still making insulin.

The game plan is to, if possible, prevent their burnout. There's a lot of evidence that high blood
sugars are toxic to beta cells and will cause more rapid burnout of beta cells.

I've had patients for over 20 years, over 30 years, still making insulin even though there were type
1 diabetics because we caught it very early and kept blood sugar as normal. That's basically the
best advice I could give.

I had a coronary artery calcium score done a number of years ago, and that was after some 70 years

### I stopped drinking alcohol about 3 months ago after experiencing a near-death experience with hypoglycemia. I noticed an overall increase in glucose readings since then and wondered if this is related to the cessation of drinking alcohol. [short: Can stopping alcohol raise blood glucose?]

It's very possible because alcohol paralyzes the liver so that it cannot perform gluconeogenesis,
the conversion of protein to glucose.

With a type 1 diabetic who's taking insulin, takes insulin for a meal, it covers the protein and the
carbohydrate in the meal.

The protein can be used to build muscle, but the excess over and above what's needed for muscle
building gets converted to glucose.

If you cannot convert it to glucose and you've taken the insulin for that protein, your blood sugar
can drop too low.

If you're drinking a lot of alcohol, you're going to prevent this from happening. You're going to
prevent the conversion of protein to glucose, and your blood sugar can drop too low. That's the
likely cause of your new problem.

### In your book, you have a diagram of insulin injection sites. Would it also be appropriate to inject into the fat pads on the bottom of the feet? [short: Injecting insulin in feet?]

Absolutely inappropriate for a variety of reasons.

One reason would be that many diabetics, after long years of poor control, will have impaired
circulation in their feet. If you injure the feet by sticking a needle into them, having impaired
circulation may impair the healing, so you may actually cause infections.

Another reason is that since you're standing on your feet, you're compressing the capillaries. Even
if you have good circulation, you're compressing the capillaries, impeding the absorption of the
insulin into the capillaries.

You're going to get not only impaired absorption, but depending upon whether you're standing or
sitting or walking, you'll get variable absorption or unpredictable absorption. There's very good
reasons not to inject into the bottom of your feet.

### I've had type 1 diabetes for 28 years. I'm 36 years old and presently have proliferative diabetic retinopathy in both eyes. My A1C is 6.5. Yes, I have, but not after they've become blind or near blind. I've repeatedly seen normalization of

If you catch it in the early stages before these blood vessels rupture and cause bleeding and

slowly reverse the proliferation, but it might take years.

### Have you seen chronic kidney disease stage 3 reversed with a 6, 12, 12 carbohydrate that's low carbohydrate way of eating? [short: Reversing stage 3 kidney disease?]

Well, what is stage 3? There are at least 2 stage 3s. There's stage 3a, which is a glomerular
filtration rate of 45 to 59. Normal is around 100 or so. Stage 4 is 15 to 29.

I've seen people with stage 3a that stayed at stage 3a for many years. This is surprising because
stage 3a means you've lost about half of your kidney function, so your kidneys are doing double
duty, and the usual picture is that when they overwork, they can deteriorate, but I've seen stage 3a

The glomerular filtration rate remained constant, so I think that jibes with the experience of
people who have donated a kidney where the donor of a kidney maintains his kidney function even

I think the reason why diabetics with stage 3a are expected to deteriorate is because the blood

normal blood sugars.

What's the rate of deterioration if you have more severe kidney disease? I don't know.

### Does low-carb keep type 2 under control? I'm getting very worried. I don't want to go on meds. [short: Type 2 without medications?]
