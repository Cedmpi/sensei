### Gastroparesis
Number seven, gastroparesis, which is a paralysis of the stomach and digestive system, which
by the way can be mild to severe in diabetes, is almost universal in long-standing diabetes,

Doctors don't realize this.

Mild cases will just show low blood sugars after some meals and higher blood sugars many

In severe gastroparesis, which is quite rare, the patient may be vomiting all day long.
I call that end-stage gastroparesis.
The next lack of information has to do with how do you diagnose gastroparesis.
The conventional wisdom is to have the patient take a gastric emptying study where they swallow

### Anything eaten can raise blood sugar
The fact that should be obvious but isn't, that carbohydrate is not the only dietary
element that raises blood sugar.
Here we have many type 1 diabetics being told to base their mealtime insulin dose on the
amount of carbohydrate they're eating and ignore all the other elements of the meal,
and within that result, of course, that most type 1 diabetics do not have consistent or
normal blood sugars after meals.

The reason that carbohydrate is not the only element that affects blood sugar, main reason
is the production of hormones by the intestines whenever you eat a meal.
And the elements of a meal that cause the production of these hormones, the most studied
one is called GLP-1.

The elements that affect the secretion of GLP-1 are carbohydrate, fat, protein and bulk.
The bigger the size of the meal, no matter what it's made of, the more GLP-1 you're going
to produce, and GLP-1 has a variety of effects upon blood sugar.
So if you took a non-diabetic and had him eat a handful of pebbles, he would make enough
insulin to offset some of the effects of the GLP-1 that his blood sugar wouldn't go up.
But you take a diabetic who cannot make insulin and feed him a handful of pebbles, just the
distention of the digestive tract from the pebbles will raise his blood sugar.

### Dawn phenomenon
The next item on our list is the dawn phenomenon.
The dawn phenomenon is a situation that affects diabetics but not non-diabetics, where they
may go to sleep with a normal blood sugar and wake up in the morning with an elevated
blood sugar, or at their usual wake-up time, their blood sugar is okay, but an hour or
two later, it may have gone up even though they did not eat any breakfast.
Now doctors, especially endocrinologists, have invented all kinds of explanations for
why this occurs, and all of the explanations but one are fantasies.

The one true explanation I have never heard from a physician or seen on the internet,
and how do I know about it, because I've been reading the scientific literature since I
got out of medical school, which was in 1983, and back around 1985, a study was done of
the dawn phenomenon, and they did it on diabetics, and whoever thought of this study, I don't
remember who it was, a pretty smart person, he speculated that because people make growth
hormone in the middle of the night, that maybe the growth hormone had to do with the dawn
phenomenon, and what they did was they measured the clearance of insulin in the bloodstream

on awakening in the morning on patients given anti-growth hormones, somatostatin, that prevents
the pituitary from making growth hormone, and they also had the same patients on other
days not receive the somatostatin, and when they received the somatostatin, these are
diabetics, when they received the somatostatin, their blood sugar went up in the morning.

When they received the somatostatin, their blood sugars did not go up, and when they
did not receive the somatostatin, their blood sugars went up, so they proved conclusively
that growth hormone is the culprit in overnight blood sugar rise, and what they found was
that people, even people who are not growing, mature adults, make a peak of growth hormone
about three hours after they go to sleep, and that growth hormone causes the liver to
have metabolic changes that take maybe four or five hours to occur where the liver is
clearing away insulin.

Now, in a non-diabetic, a non-diabetic can make at least three times as much insulin
as they'll ever need.
Diabetics, however, have injected their long-acting insulin at bedtime, and it's being trickled
into their bloodstream in small amounts overnight, and as this slow insulin infusion occurs in
the early morning, the liver is able to clear most of it away so their blood sugars go up.

### Half-life of insulin
Another fact that doctors don't know is the half-life of insulin in your bloodstream.
You might ask your doctor, if he were to inject insulin into your vein, how long would
it be detectable in your blood, and there'll be speculation because they have no idea.

The real truth, which has been studied and reported, but doctors don't read the research
literature, is that insulin has a half-life in the blood of six and a half minutes.
If you inject 10 units into a vein in six and a half minutes, there will be only five
units detectable in the blood, and in five half-lives, there'll be none detectable in
the blood.

Now what actually happens is perhaps 10% of the injected insulin finds receptors on cells
throughout the body on which the insulin acts and binds the receptors early on.
It takes a while once insulin has hit a receptor for the insulin to do its job, which is to
bring glucose into the cell.
So early on, the insulin gets bound, but takes a long while to have its effect, and in the
meanwhile, the concentration in the blood is being cleared away by the liver quite rapidly,
half-life six and a half minutes.
