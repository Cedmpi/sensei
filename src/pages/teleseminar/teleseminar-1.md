### Special Subject: Insulin secretion can be restored after in a non-diabetogenic environment

The first one is, and I quote from the article in the journal Diabetes,
at onset of type 1 diabetes, insulin secretion can be restored after some days in a non-diabetogenic environment.
This refers to an article published in the July 15th issue of Diabetes,
a journal of the American Diabetes Association, volume 64, number 7.

In this study, they actually were able to recruit some diabetics who, after they had died,
who died immediately after the onset of type 1 diabetes due to things like motor vehicle accidents and so on,
and they were able to extract living beta cells from their pancreases,

and they cultured them or grew them in culture media,
and what they found was that they were not producing normal phase 1 and phase 2 insulin responses
in response to glucose in the medium.

But if they grew them for several days in a medium that was free of glucose,
that was, as they called it, non-hyperglycemic,
the beta cells recovered their phase 1 and phase 2 insulin responses,
and this supports the many studies that have shown that if you rapidly treat early diabetes with normal blood sugars,
you can get some degree of beta cell recovery.

And so this is a very graphic demonstration and just reiterates what we've seen in larger scale studies
that did not involve taking out islets, but here we see this happening in a very dramatic way.

### Special Subject: Reproducibility of blood sugar meter
The next special subject we have relates to how you can check the reproducibility of your blood sugar meter
and even possibly the accuracy.
As you probably know, most of the blood sugar meters on the market,
according to studies that have already been published, are grossly inaccurate.

I seek the best accuracy, especially around normal values,
so I consider normal values around 80, except in children in the 70s,
and how do you do that?

Well, one could say, let's look at the control solutions or test solutions that the manufacturers provide with their meters.
The trouble is that nowadays most of the manufacturers are not providing normal controls.
It has to do with some FDA guidelines that don't make sense,
and they're doing the minimum that they have to do to comply with FDA regulations,
so they usually have control solutions that would read somewhere around 30 or 40 mg/dL on the one end
and another solution that would read around 300 mg/dL.

Now, I'm not interested in precise accuracy at a blood sugar of 30 or at a blood sugar of 300.
I want it accurate where most of my patients' blood sugars are,
which would be, let's say, between 80 and 100.
So how do I get this and how do I go about testing?

Well, what we do is we get hold of the manufacturer's control solutions for a meter,
and we use three parts of the low control, which might be 30 or 40 as the middle of the range,
and one part of the high control, and we'd put them in a little plastic squeeze bottle
or in a glass dropper bottle that has a tight seal because you don't want evaporation.

Evaporation will make the concentration of glucose go up,
and this mixture will have a blood sugar of somewhere around 100.
It might be 108, it might be 113, it might be 97,
and you could calculate what that value would be by taking the middle value of the low control.
So let's say the low control is 30 to 70.

You'd get the middle value by adding 30 and 70 and dividing by 2, and you get 50.
And if the high control is 200 to 400, the middle value would be 300,
so we would take three parts of the low control, so 3 times 50 would be 150,
and we'd add in one high control, which would be 300, so that adds up to 450,
and we've taken a total of four samples, three in one, so we divide 450 by four,
and you get something like 112.

So this solution should read 112 mg/dL.
So if the solution is accurate, if the manufacturer has provided you with accurate control solutions,
then it's a good test of the accuracy of the meter.
It should read that value that I just told you, I think I said 104.
Now, just as important or more important is the reproducibility.

What if you did 10 tests in a row, would they all read 104?
And what we find with most of these meters is that they're scattered over a wide range.
In fact, we found that one of the most popular selling meters,
a meter that many of the insurance companies require for diabetics instead of the most accurate one,
reads chronically low and may read very low or very haphazardly.
So with that particular meter, you might think you're going to get a reading of 104 and you get 160,
or you get 50.

That's how wide a range of inaccuracy you get with some meters,
and therefore you do five or ten tests in a row, you get a load of different numbers.
On other meters, like the one that we recommend, and we've mentioned it before,
we get the same value over and over and over again, plus or minus one or two mg/dL.
So that shows consistency.

And then if our reading is almost on the nose with the value of our control solution,
we can be fairly competent of the accuracy, but we're assuming that the manufacturer made accurate control solutions.
So the only way to be absolutely sure of the accuracy is to do a blood sugar at the clinical lab
while they're drawing blood from your arm and see how it compares.
