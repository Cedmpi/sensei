### Special subject: Hypothyroidism impairs human stem cell derived pancreatic progenitor cell maturation in mice

Sounds very complicated, but the net result is pretty simple.
If a mouse has low thyroid hormone production, the cells that eventually become beta cells
that make insulin instead become alpha cells that make glucagon.

So apparently normal thyroid function or normal blood thyroid levels, free T3 and free T4
in the bloodstream are essential for the maturation of beta cells so that they become
insulin producing and not glucagon producing.

Now therefore, hypothyroidism could be one of a number of causes of diabetes.
Type 1 or type 2, unclear to me which since both type 1 and type 2 involve loss of beta
cells.

Furthermore, if a patient is newly diagnosed with diabetes and therefore still has functioning
beta cells, it would be nice if he could continue to make beta cells.
So you want to make sure that he is not hypothyroid and we've taught how to diagnose hypothyroidism.

We've taught especially the importance of measuring free T3 and if necessary giving
both T3 and T4 supplements.

Now we'll get on to the questions.

### Hard lumps/slash fat deposits that can develop as a result of injections. Are they caused more by the number or frequency of injections or due to the amount of insulin being injected? [short: Cause of lumps from injections?]

Well, first of all, there are several reasons for lumps on the skin and hard lumps usually
come because the injection was not deep enough, if it was intradermal rather than subcutaneous,
not in the subcutaneous fat but just above the fat.

So then you have a blob of insulin that's sitting there barely under your skin and that'll
feel hard.
Another situation that can give a hard lump is if you inject a very large dose as the
ADA recommends, it might form a big blob of insulin that will feel hard for the time
being until it's absorbed.

But there are other kinds of lumps that occur.
For example, insulin is a kind of growth hormone.
It stimulates the production of fat and it's long been known that if you keep injecting
insulin into the same area, you might cause fat growth.

So for example, when I first saw people using insulin pumps where they had the needle insertion
on the sides of their hips, they looked like they had wings growing out of their sides
because the insulin had stimulated the growth of fat.

There's another kind of lumpiness that injections can cause.
In this case, it's not ordinary injections but the use of insulin pumps.
Leave a sizable piece of tubing under your skin for several days and then you put another
piece under your skin for another several days in the same general area and these foreign
bodies cause what's called a foreign body reaction and the body makes scar tissue in
response to it.

On top of that, the insulin pumps are continually injecting insulin into one spot while they're
connected and insulin is a foreign body.
It doesn't belong in the skin.
So if it's going into one place day in and day out for hours, it can also cause a foreign
body reaction.

So you have both the growth of fat due to the hormone effect and you have the foreign
body reaction which can cause scar tissue growth.
The net result in so far as insulin pumps goes is that eventually there's so much scar
tissue that the absorption of insulin becomes grossly impaired and unpredictable and that's
one reason why I don't support the use of insulin pumps.

For a few months until you develop the scar tissue, it might work fine but eventually
there are problems.

### You're against insulin pumps because of possible scar tissue. Since the continuous glucose monitor uses an introducer needle to insert a cannula through the skin like an insulin pump, does that mean you do not recommend CGM? [short: CGM scar tissue formation?]

Well, you're assuming the wrong thing.
The continuous glucose monitor does not insert a cannula.
A cannula would be a sizable piece of tubing with a hole in it through which fluid flows.

Instead, the continuous glucose monitor inserts a fiber that may be say three times the thickness
of a human hair.
You can barely see it.
If you touch your finger with it, you probably can't feel it.
I certainly can't feel it because I had all of the not the scars but the calluses
on my fingertips from all the blood sugars I've done.
No way can I feel that microscopic fiber.

So that fiber is so tiny that it's not going to cause scar tissue formation and there is
not fluid flowing into the skin through that fiber.
It's actually sucking out interstitial fluid via capillary action so you're not putting
things into the skin, you're pulling things out of the skin, interstitial fluid.

This same person brings up the fact that the FDA has approved a new continuous glucose
monitor and where the manufacturer says you no longer have to check your blood sugar and
that is utter nonsense.

To calibrate the monitor, you must check your blood sugar and although the new monitor may
be more accurate than the old one, there's an underlying problem.
It is measuring interstitial fluid glucose, not blood glucose and the glucose gets into
the interstitial fluid 20 to 30 minutes after the blood sugar.

So there's a time delay and if your blood sugar is changing rapidly, you might get into
trouble.
For example, you might be dropping very rapidly and not catch it when it gets very low because
it's reading a prior blood sugar, namely 30 minutes earlier.
