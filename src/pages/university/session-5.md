Now, how did this happen? Or why did it happen?

She must have had some sort of illness that was causing the vomiting. Let's call it a gastroenteritis.
It could have been bacterial, it could have been viral, but if you are diabetic and you develop an infection,
whether it's bacterial or viral, chances are that your blood sugar is going to go up.
And once your blood sugar is elevated, you become insulin resistant and the insulin you injected,
or if you're a type 2 diabetic, the insulin you make, is not going to work as well when your blood sugar is higher than when it's lower.

By way of example, it may take 5 times as much insulin to get a blood sugar of 650 down to 600 as it would take to get 150 down to 100.
So, you're starting the day off with insulin resistance and your blood sugar, even if you're injecting insulin, basal insulin, is going to go up.
Now, if your blood sugar goes up high enough, you're going to start peeing and losing fluid.

But this woman was also vomiting, and she was losing fluid by vomiting and was unable to replace it.
So, now she had two reasons to lose fluid, peeing and vomiting.
Now, you may remember from high school biology that they showed you a picture, a silhouette of a human body with a big heart,
and giant blood vessels coming out of the heart and getting smaller and smaller as they get to the fingers and toes.
While these small capillaries account for the bulk of the blood flow throughout the body, not just in the fingers and toes.
The bulk of our circulation is in small blood vessels.

And if you're dehydrated, losing fluid, like from vomiting, your blood volume is going down because your blood is mostly water.
And you're not going to be able to deliver insulin and glucose to the peripheral tissues at the end of the line
where these little blood vessels no longer have blood because you're dehydrated.
So, the cells at the end of the line are going to be starving, but they have an option for staying alive.

You may recall from an earlier session that in the absence of insulin, remember that you're dehydrated,
you're not delivering blood, insulin and glucose to the tissues near the end of the line.
If you have not enough insulin, insulin-sensitive lipase, an enzyme that metabolizes fats, is going to be activated.
And you're going to start converting your fats in these peripheral tissues to ketones.

When you metabolize fats and do it at the extent that we're doing it now because you have nothing left to keep your life,
or to keep tissues alive, you're going to make ketones and the kidneys love to pee away ketones.

So now, you're vomiting and losing fluid, you're peeing away glucose because your blood sugars are high,
and you're peeing away ketones because you're metabolizing the fats because you don't have enough insulin.
So, all of this boils down to high blood sugars, not enough insulin, but it ends up in severe dehydration.
And that's how this lady ended up in this state.
Now, what do we do about it?

Let's take three scenarios.  
First scenario, you wake up, you feel nauseated, and you throw up, and now you feel better.
If you're a patient of mine, I tell you to call me right away.
But if you're without a doctor who knows what to do, you want to consume enough fluid to handle another vomiting episode if you get one later in the day.
And you shouldn't eat breakfast, maybe you shouldn't eat lunch if you're in touch with your doctor.
Negotiate with him if you're hungry at lunchtime, whether it's okay to have lunch.

A person who weighs 140 pounds (64kg) and only threw up once probably should consume two and a half to three quarts of fluid in the next 24 hours.
If you're out of the USA, you consume two and a half to three liters of fluid for someone who weighs about 140 pounds (64kg).
Now, that was easy.  
The doctor should be available so that you should be able to contact him if you get sick later on.

Next scenario, you wake up, you feel nauseated, you throw up, and you don't feel better.
This is a lot more serious. This is what happened to this lady. She threw up and then she kept throwing up.
And for these people, I have provided all of my patients at the very first visit to my office with injectable Tigan, which is a medication that stops vomiting.
And I will tell the patient to inject Tigan, you inject it like insulin, you use it in an insulin syringe, and the amount depends on your weight, and your doctor has to figure that out for you.

But it's essential to have this in your possession so that if you are vomiting or if you have a child who's vomiting, you have the Tigan available.
We used to have Tigan suppositories, but because they were never tested in clinical trials, the FDA ended up taking them off the market.
But we do know that the injectable Tigan usually works. I have not had a case where it didn't work. In theory, it is possible to overdose on Tigan, which can give you neurologic adverse effects.

But the antidote to these adverse effects is benadryl. If you're out of the USA, they call it diphenhydramine, and you can buy it in a liquid in any drug store over the counter, and one gulp of benadryl should stop the side effects of Tigan.

Now, let's assume that the Tigan works, and here you are at home with the bottle of Tigan. What else do you do?
Well, if you're type 1 diabetic, you must take your basal insulin, and if you don't know what basal and bolus insulin dosing is, you get my book Diabetes Solution.

You check your blood sugar every 5 or 6 hours to see if it goes too high, and you might take an intramuscular shot of fast-acting insulin to bring you down rapidly.
