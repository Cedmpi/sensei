This is Dr. Richard Bernstein with Session 9 of Dr. Bernstein's Diabetes University.
In this session, we're going to be discussing again dehydrating illness,
which we did a few sessions back when we talked about vomiting.

Now we have a subject that's
almost as much fun as vomiting, and that is diarrhea. Now, I'm not talking about the severe,
dangerous diarrheas, bloody diarrheas with a fever that you may get from travel or unique
exposures. I'm talking about the day-to-day thing that's very common, especially in children,
but that almost everyone experiences every few years. And I'll show you how we handle it.

Now let's say that a patient of mine has an episode of very loose stools, maybe even explosive.
I tell him that he shouldn't bother me until he has a second episode. Why on earth would I
tell her that? Because many people will have something for dinner that was spoiled. They'll
get diarrhea, they'll get the material out of their intestines, and they'll feel better.

But if they have a second episode, I'm worried because then they might have a third and a fourth and get
severely dehydrated. So if someone has a second episode, I say, call me immediately. What do I
tell them? Number one, I ask for other symptoms just to make sure that it's only simple diarrhea,
usually due to a gastrointestinal virus. On rare occasions, it might be a bacterial enteritis,
but I want to make sure it's not something special.

If you have bloody stool or a fever, the ballgame changes dramatically, and I'm not going to discuss that kind of ballgame.
So if someone calls me and they've had a second episode of diarrhea, I will remind them that I
told them at their first visit to purchase Lomotil, L-O-M-O-T-I-L. Generically, it's
diphenoxylate. And what's special about diphenoxylate, it also has atropine in it,
what's special about it is that it comes in liquid as well as tablets. Why am I interested
in liquid? Simply because it works faster than the tablets.

So I have every new patient before I see them for the first time purchase diphenoxylate plus atropine liquid and tablets. The liquid is
intended for infants and it's administered to infants with a plastic dropper that's marked
at two and a half ml and five ml, which also corresponds to two and a half milligrams and
five milligrams. For an adult, I might have them take 10 or 20 droppers full right away,
which would be a very high dose for an infant, but is probably around the right dose for an
adult.

For a five-year-old child, we might try a few droppers full. It's all trial and error.
One might ask, well, what about adverse effects? I've only had one person out of thousands get
adverse effects and that was me. I had a severe diarrhea from a separate condition that we'll talk
about in another session called CVID and I took 37 tablets because I had to testify as an expert
witness in court. Well, the 37 tablets dried out my intestines, but also dried out my larynx and
when I tried to speak, nothing came out, but I'm the only person I know of who had a side effect.

So we start with a liquid low modal and we take a number of droppers full, many droppers full for
an adult and a few for a child and we have to wait for it to work. It might take 45 minutes to
an hour. I usually give it 45 minutes to see some effect and if we see no effect after 45 minutes,
I'll double the prior dose and we'll keep doing that until we get to a dose that works, but we
give it at least 45 minutes to give it a chance. Once we know what dose worked, we know that each
two and a half milliliters on the dropper corresponds to one tablet, the two and a half
milligram tablets and we will wait three or four hours and give that number of tablets.

Try to extend the time between tablets because you may be getting better. Usually these episodes
of diarrhea last about 24 hours. So by the time you get the dose initially adjusted, you might
want to try, let's say you started taking tablets every three hours, you might see if you can get
every three and a half hours without getting more diarrhea and if that works, you try for every four
hours. If that works, every four and a half hours. 

Eventually, if you wait long enough, you'll get to the point where the diarrhea has gone away. So that's the ballgame. Now as far
as fluid replacement, it's exactly the same as fluid replacement was when we were dealing with
vomiting. So we're going to have to drink for let's say a 140 pound (64 kg) person something like a minimum
of two and a half liters of spiked fluid per day and I told you in the prior session how to spike
it. I'll repeat it which is a level teaspoon of table salt per quart and a quarter teaspoon of
the potassium salt which is sold for people with hypertension usually comes in a blue cardboard box.

I'll show it to you again. Nope. Here it is. So a quarter teaspoon of potassium salt per quart of
fluid. Fluid could be water, it could be diet soda or you could take sugar-free Gatorade without any
spiking, no extra salts or you could take mineral water without any extra spiking. Remember that if
you have bloody diarrhea or a fever, you should see a physician and taking the low modal may not
be a good idea because you might want to get the toxic material out of your system.

I've heard many gastroenterologists over the years telling people that if you have diarrhea you can still eat.
Yet I've by the similar token heard the spouses of patients tell me he only has diarrhea when he eats or she only
has diarrhea when she eats. So I've learned from my patients or at least from their spouses that
you shouldn't feed somebody who has diarrhea. You stand a good chance of making it worse. So stay
away from food. The most bouts of diarrhea last only a day or two so you're not likely to starve
anyone. If it becomes a matter of starvation then things are much more serious than we're discussing
here.
