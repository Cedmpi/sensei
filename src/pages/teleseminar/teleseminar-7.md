### Special subject: Fasting
First, we have a special
subject today, which relates to the current wave of literature, TV specials, etc. on fasting.
A lot of physicians, psychologists, amateurs, physical trainers are recommending that people
fast to lose weight. There are even people recommending that diabetics should fast to
make their diabetes better. I've had a little bit of experience in this realm now, because
some of my patients have tried different fasting regimens.

And what we see is variable results.
For example, I have one person who cut himself down to 600 calories a day for a month, lost
a lot of weight, I think he lost somewhere around 40 pounds (18 kg), which was a huge amount.
But he could only do it for a month and rapidly regain the weight. I had another patient who
had the notion that he could cut out lunch and breakfast every other day. And lo and
behold, he said he was no longer hungry at dinner time. So he's been doing this now for
several months, has lost quite a bit of weight, and is continuing and has no temptation to
snack, which is what he had been doing before, nor overeat.

So for this fellow, this form
of fasting has worked. I imagine that different forms of fasting will work for different people.
And no form of fasting may work for some people, that is, some people may
be immune to benefiting from fasting.

There's one little catch when it comes to diabetes.
If you are intervening in your routine, you're going to have to change your medication doses.
And there's the possibility, especially if you're taking insulin, that you might develop
hypoglycemia if you cut out too much food or don't reduce your medications. So it becomes
tricky if you have diabetes. 

If you've read my book, Diabetes Solution, you've become more of
a professional at adjusting medications, and therefore you may be able to successfully fast
in some manner without getting into trouble. But you have to use your head and know what you're
doing.

### Four year old with diabetes and celiac disease
*My four year old was diagnosed three weeks ago with type one diabetes and celiac disease 12 months ago. I've put her on a low carb diet and have been
keeping her in a range between 50 and 108 milligrams per deciliter. They want to let me
give her Levemir and am I doing any danger by not giving her Levemir and just staying on the
diet?*

Well, that's a good question. One danger with Levemir insulin in a very small patient is
that it's not dilutable as the old Ultralente was, which was an even longer acting insulin,
and we could dilute it for children. Levemir cannot be diluted.

So there's the uncertainty
of getting too much of an effect. You can only adjust the dose in one unit increments. One unit
of Levemir may be worth about 60 on my blood sugar, spread over the course of 24 hours, of
course. But for someone who weighs, let's say, a fifth of my weight, one unit can lower my blood
sugar. It's five times 60 or 300 over the course of 24 hours, if it were one unit more than I
needed. So there's trouble with adjusting the dose.

Therefore, for very small children, we
prefer to use NPH insulin, which while not really long acting, it's intermediate acting, can be
diluted. But you would have to give it probably anywhere from three to six times per day to cover
the whole day. Because it's really not long acting. 

I would say probably as a guess, four
times a day for a little child, but it would have to be diluted. And one can dilute insulin
with diluting fluid provided by the manufacturer. And you could use all kinds of ratios. For example,
if you dilute it one to one, one part diluting fluid to one part insulin, you've cut the potency
of the insulin in half. 

I use regular insulin for correcting elevated blood sugar that I've
diluted nine to one. So one unit only has a 10th of the effect that the pure insulin would have.
And I have some children getting insulin diluted 15 to one and even 19 to one. So it takes some
experimentation. It takes my book to show you how to go about step by step how to go
about diluting. If you don't want to do it yourself, you can get compounding chemists to
dilute insulin. But the chances are that this little kid is going to need diluted insulin.

The
benefit to taking insulin is that you help perpetuate the honeymoon period. You keep more
beta cells alive for a longer period of time. And when in the rare cases where I've been the
one to diagnose a new patient to discover a new diabetic, if I immediately keep their blood
sugars absolutely normal, I preserve much of their beta cell function. So that makes life
much easier down the road. 

It's much easier to treat someone who's still making insulin. So if
you take this child whose blood sugars are going as high as 108, and most children have blood
sugars averaging in the 70s, we narrow that a little so that her blood sugar is, 
at a high of maybe 82 at this age, we can hopefully preserve some beta cells. So there
would be benefit to insulin. But the Levemir might or might not be too potent. You could experiment,
but I'd suggest you read my book first. Diabetes Solution.

###  Why is Levemir the best in your opinion? 

I didn't say Levemir was the best. There
are pros and cons to the various long-acting insulins. The intermediate NPH has the advantage
it can be diluted. The main competitor to Levemir is Lantus. And Lantus, likewise cannot be diluted.
But it also has the potential hazard for predisposing for cancer. In fact, I believe it
was Novo who first came up with the formula for Lantus and then decided not to proceed because it
had something like 165 times the affinity for cancer cells for IGF-1 receptors, I should say,
which are found on cancer cells. 165 times the affinity than human insulin had, therefore
suggesting a potential risk. And indeed, studies in Europe show that in some countries they found
considerable risk. In other countries that had different modes of record-keeping, they may not
have had any risk.

So there's the big suspicion that Lantus may be associated with increased
rates of cancer. Why take that chance when you have something that doesn't have such an
association? Also, a number of people have complained that Lantus burned when they injected
it. It tends to be acid and may burn a little. That leaves us with several long-acting insulins
that are highly potent, a U300, a U500. We don't need such potent insulins much harder to measure
your dose.

They're born of the habits of professional diabetes association practitioners
to feed people a lot of carbohydrate and use basal insulin not just as a basal insulin but
also for covering food. So they give them a lot of carbohydrate, they give them a very potent,
super potent insulin and give them huge doses. But they're mechanically smaller doses because
they're so concentrated. We don't want those insulins.

There's a new insulin, Tresiba, which
is a very good possibility because it lasts overnight. And the Lantus and the Levemir for
most type 1 patients does not last a full night because of the dawn phenomenon. You have to take
it twice a day and the nighttime dose usually does not last until you get up in the morning.
So people tend to wake up with high blood sugars when they take these insulins. Therefore,
Tresiba is a real possibility and I'm using it on many of my patients.

### Can statins lower blood sugars ?

*Can high cholesterol be a symptom of high blood sugars and if you take a statin will your blood sugars also come down?*

Well high cholesterol is associated with high blood sugars. It's also associated
with low thyroid. But we now know from modern studies that risk of heart attacks is not
associated with cholesterol levels. And in fact the at least in older women where the study was
done cholesterol death risk is a U-shaped curve where over a certain value increase risk of death
and under a certain value increases risk of death. So low cholesterol can be dangerous.

Although statins are being used to lower LDL the reason they lower cardiac death risk in some
people in a small percentage of the users is because they are anti-inflammatory. They lower
inflammatory mediators and that's now believed the reason for their effectiveness.
Now it happens that high blood sugars also increase inflammatory mediators as does
abdominal fat. A statin will not lower your blood sugars but it has been found that statins
increase the risk of diabetes by a little over 20 percent. So statins might raise blood sugar.
