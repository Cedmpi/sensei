### Type 1 daughter became hypoglycemic?
*My 17-year-old daughter was diagnosed with type 1 when she was nine. She has been having three weeks of continual
lows at night with no insulin on board. I've had her off Lantus since Mother's Day. For some
reason, glucose barely raises her, and she sometimes had to consume 200 plus carbs before
her blood sugar rises, only to fall again 30 minutes later. Her endocrinologist did not know
what is going on, and her thyroid levels that we tested were normal. Need your help.*

This sounds
very strange. A type 1 diabetic who becomes not only cured of diabetes, but hypoglycemic. Now,
years ago, before it was possible to measure C-peptide, there was a rule of thumb that doctors
were supposed to use if someone is getting frequent hypoglycemia and they're not diabetic,
and one was surreptitious use of insulin. This phenomenon was most common in nurses who could
get hold of insulin, and they'd give themselves injected insulin and then go to the doctors
because they had hyperglycemia.

So, surreptitious use of insulin is probably the most common cause
of mysterious hyperglycemia, and an insulinoma would be another cause. So, insulinomas are tumors
that produce insulin. They usually are not malignant, but they can give you a hyperglycemia,
and the presence of an insulinoma can be determined by measuring serum C-peptide level
because commercial insulin does not contain C-peptide, and if you're making a lot of insulin,
your serum C-peptide is going to be high. I've explained in the past what C-peptide is. I even
drew a diagram. It's manufactured at the same time as insulin is manufactured by the body. So,
a C-peptide test is justified.

One other possibility which I find hard to really consider
would be some kind of damage to the liver where it is not adequately clearing away insulin or
raising blood sugar when blood sugar is low. If the liver is functioning normally, the half-life
of insulin in the bloodstream is six and a half minutes. So, if you were to inject ten units of
regular insulin, say human insulin, directly into a vein, in six and a half minutes,
only 5 units are left in your bloodstream. 

In 13 minutes, only two and a half units. In 5
half-lives, there's no detectable insulin left in your bloodstream. So, the liver works very
rapidly to get rid of any excess insulin in the bloodstream. So, one could investigate a liver
function, but I'd first check C-peptide. 

Then there's a question of why
was the diabetes cured? Sometimes people with polycystic ovarian syndrome can have a cure that
can spontaneously improve. Sometimes the medications used to treat PCOS can reverse
the diabetes. It's hard for me to think of any other reason for the diabetes being cured. The
first guess is always surreptitious use of insulin.


### SGLT2 agonists
*With the news coming from the
American Diabetes Association about the SGLT2 drugs not only reducing blood sugars, blood
pressure, and weight, but also reducing the risk of heart attacks and sudden deaths by 38%. What
is your opinion on this for your type 2 patients who are not at goal?*

I use the SGLT2 agonists as
a last resort. Why are they losing weight? They're losing weight because they're losing fluid.
They're peeing a lot. And if they drink adequate fluid as you're supposed to when you take this
class of drugs, you won't lose the weight, but you have to drink a hell of a lot of fluid not
to lose that weight. And as far as blood pressure effect, because you're diuresing and losing fluid,
you're reducing blood volume and lowering your blood pressure. So these are artificial effects
from peeing a lot. 

This class of drug achieves its effect by causing you to pee away glucose
when your blood sugar is lower than the usual renal threshold. So instead of peeing away
glucose at a blood sugar of 165, you may be peeing away glucose at a blood sugar of 135.
It's not really the way of doing the game that I would like because there are side effects,
namely dehydration. I've had patients with very high hematocrits, that is their red cell
concentration goes up and can increase the risk of clogging small blood vessels, causing a variety
of vascular damages. I don't consider it a wholly safe product.

And there are other oral agents that
are much safer. But I have one patient who requires a lot of insulin. He has a number of
problems and says that the only time he gets normal blood sugars is when he, he's a type 2,
is when he takes an SGLT to agonist. And I prescribe it for him and he's doing fine,
but he knows he has to drink a lot of water.

### Best exercise reducing stored muscular sugar?
*I would like to know which exercise system is best for
reducing stored muscular sugar. High repetition, low loading or low repetition, maximum loading?*

This question is utter nonsense. There's no sugar stored in muscles. And what is stored is
glycogen which is starch. And you want it stored in muscles because it could be a lifesaver if you
develop hypoglycemia or if you are starving or if you stop eating for a day or so.

So muscles store
glycogen and the liver also stores glycogen. I believe about two-thirds of the body's glycogen
is stored in the liver and about one-third in the muscles. I imagine, however, if a person were very
muscular, he might store more than a third in his muscles, might store half or even two-thirds.

So you don't want to get rid of the glycogen in your muscles. You want to replace it
with normal, by having normal blood sugars if you lose it. When do you lose it? Well,
if you're running a marathon, you're going to lose it pretty rapidly,
so you have to be drinking glucose drinks while you're running, etc.

### Handling eating out with others
*How do you handle the need for specific timing plus limited food choices when eating with others?*
