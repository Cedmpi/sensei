### Special subject: Adverse effects of oral anti-diabetic agents
Our special subject
for today is update on adverse effects of oral anti-diabetic agents. Not all bad but
physicians should be cautious. Now it's interesting. I keep encountering coincidences almost every
day in my life. I decided on this subject yesterday. Last night I got the current issue
of the Journal of the American Medical Association and their feature article is entitled comparison
of clinical outcomes and adverse events associated with glucose-lowering drugs in patients with
type 2 diabetes. Here's the article. 

Now it's very interesting that the conclusions
of this Cochrane survey of hundreds of studies of oral anti-diabetic agents were that the
and they compared them mostly to metformin which was sort of a gold standard and their
comparison was that adverse effects were minimal and almost not different from metformin
for just about everything. My experience does not quite jive with this and what I'm going
to talk about is my experience. I'm going to go down each of the oral agents that are
used to treat type diabetes.

The first one is a class called alpha-glucosidase inhibitors.
This includes Acarbose, Glyset and other similar products which are intended to inhibit the
digestion of simple polysaccharides that is sugars that are not pure simple sugars
like glucose or fructose but are maybe 2 or 3 glucose molecules stuck together or things
like what you see in bread starches. 

Here we have the American Diabetes Association
advocating that you eat a lot of starches and then they want you to take an alpha-glucosidase
inhibitor to stop you from digesting the starches because they know the starches are going to
raise your blood sugar. It's sort of crazy. Their favorite starch is whole grain breads
but they also have fruits in there and the alpha-glucosidase inhibitors will reduce the
adverse effects on blood sugar of eating these foods, will not eliminate it, will not greatly
eliminate it but will somewhat reduce these effects.

The only adverse effect of the alpha-glucosidase
inhibitors stems from the fact that you end up with these simple polysaccharides getting
into the lower intestines where bacteria can digest them, create methane and possibly other
gases and cause flatulence. And the only hazard of flatulence is if you happen to be standing
near an open flame, you can experience what the Italians would call un'esplosione come un colpo di cannone.
So if you're using these alpha-glucosidase inhibitors which make no
sense to begin with, just stay away from an open flame.

The next category I'll mention is
thiazolidinedione, that's Actos and Avandia. For a while, there was a big scare about bladder cancer
which is a very rare condition and so rare that with or without the thiazolidinediones,
it was almost impossible to find. And eventually, the scare was removed but people are still asking
questions in my teleseminars about the hazards of bladder cancer. There ain't no hazards. Several
studies have been unable to find any association. There are mild associations with bone loss or
bone fractures and there's still a warning associated with congestive heart failure which
has, I believe, recently been retracted but I am not retracting it fully because both Avandia and
Actos but mostly Actos by far foster fluid retention in the body. And if you already have
congestive heart failure, your heart is too weak to pump a lot of fluid around your body,
a lot of blood. So one of the treatments for CHF is a diuretic to make you pee away fluid. But
Actos causes fluid retention. So it might be hazardous for people who already have congestive
heart failure.

I happen to be personally prejudiced against excess fluid retention. So I teach my
patients who use Actos to check for edema or swelling in the legs. And we do this for a little
test where you press on your tibia looking for what's called pretibial edema. And this test is
demonstrated in one of my videos. So if you go through the hundred or more videos that I have
on YouTube and on Facebook, you can see my test for pretibial edema. So if I put someone on
Actos, number one, I know in advance that they don't have congestive heart failure. I wouldn't
use it on such people. And two, I don't want them to get swelling in their legs. 

My reason for
prejudice against this swelling is that I was director of the wound care clinic at a major
university hospital. I worked there for 27 years. And the most common wounds of the legs that we
saw were venous stasis ulcers. And these are caused by fluid retention in the legs. And that's
why I'm prejudiced against fluid retention in the legs. In any event, you have to check for
pretibial edema. And if you're taking Actos, and to a lesser degree, if you're taking Avandia,
your doctor should teach you how to check for pretibial edema.

The next category is sulfonylurea drugs and glinides, which are similar to sulfonylurea.
These are drugs that cause the body to make more insulin if it's capable of making insulin. Now,
there are some problems with this. According to the ADA, which still classifies these as major
treatment options, and cite as the only adverse effect possibility of hypoglycemia, if you use
them incorrectly. But there's another effect that they don't mention. Back in the 1970s, I guess,
there was a major study of the sulfonylureas because they were the only oral agent we had
for treating type 2 diabetes. It was called the University Studies Group. And this study
showed a very substantial increase in heart attacks amongst the people who took sulfonylureas.

There was some question about the accuracy of the study, but the numbers seemed to be pretty
overwhelming. So certainly, low carbohydrate diets would have been much more effective than
the sulfonylureas, but people weren't going to try low carbohydrate diets then. They were worried
about fats. So here we had the entire diabetes profession was switching to sulfonylureas because
there was nothing else available. And since that time, there have been repeated studies of
association with heart attacks done in a variety of countries over the years.

And these don't get
mentioned in the Cochrane study that's being quoted in the Journal of the American Medical
Association this week. I don't understand it. These studies have been repeated and sulfonylureas
and I assume because they're similar, the glinides are hazardous and should be avoided. So I stopped
prescribing those things after I was in practice only a few years because of the hazards associated
with them.

Next on the list is the GLP-1 agonists, glucagon-like peptide-1 agonists,
Bieta, Victoza, etc. I've discussed these at great lengths on my teleseminar and also on my
Dr. Bernstein's Diabetes University videos and they have very little effect directly upon blood
sugar, but they can have considerable effect upon appetite. 

The major adverse effect is that they
slow digestion which could be good because if you're a type 2 diabetic, your slow insulin
production can cope with slow stomach emptying better than it can with rapid digestion. So you
might say that slow stomach emptying is an advantage also, but they can cause constipation,
maybe nausea, but these are rare, relatively rare and mild.

However, there is a package
insert warning about pancreatitis and everyone is pretending that the pancreatitis is so rare
that you can not worry about it. Well, I was worried about it. So when I put a patient on
a GLP-1 agonist, I test them for pancreatitis and how do I do that? The next time I draw bloods,
I measure their pancreatic lipase and pancreatic amylase and the more important one is the lipase
and what I found is over the course of a year or so, three people developed elevations.

Two of
those people developed symptoms. One of them developed very severe symptoms and was about
to go to a gastroenterologist when I called her and told her to stop the product and sure enough,
her symptoms went away and not only did she have severe GI distress, but her serum protein dropped
dramatically and this is characteristic of pancreatitis where the injured pancreas is
leaking protein into the abdominal cavity so that the total serum protein level drops off.

So here in one year, the handful of people I had put on these agents were three of them got
pancreatitis. So my warning is yes, you can use GLP-1 agonists by Victoza, Amylin,
Symlin and so on, but the doctor should be testing the patient periodically for at least
pancreatic lipase and if you find any elevation of lipase, you have to stop the product right away.
Don't even assume it's due to another cause. Stop the product and see what happens at the lipase
level.

Next is the DPP-4 antagonists. This is a class of drugs like Januvia and there's a group
of them that are similar. These drugs inhibit the enzyme that destroys GLP-1 and the product
is relatively benign, but it has virtually no effect on blood sugar. Why the FDA approves
something that has no effect on blood sugar, I don't know. However, there is some evidence
that they foster beta cell recovery and therefore on my teleseminars, I was advocating the use of
DPP-4 agonists to foster beta cell recovery and then came some reports that they increased the
incidence of heart failure.

So I advised on my teleseminar, you better not do this. Don't use
them and I advised that a month ago, but lo and behold, we now have new studies that have come out
that have relinquished the inhibition of the DPP-4 agonists on account of heart failure. They
supposedly do not cause heart failure. So I now can recommend them again for helping to foster
beta cell recovery in a newly diagnosed diabetic or in a borderline patient or a pre-diabetic
patient, they can foster beta cell recovery. So DPP-4 agonists would be back on my book for that
purpose.

One thing I forgot to mention when I was talking about the GLP-1 agonists, their only
purpose as far as I'm concerned is to curb appetite and we've discussed that at length.
They can be very effective, but for many people, they stop working after a few months. So it's not
like they're an absolute answer to curbing appetite, but they may be effective.

I also
should point out that the GLP-1 agonists that are sold to take once a week, are not focused
on the time of the day when you're overeating, so they're less likely to adequately affect your
appetite. You're getting their effect all day long and you're going to be developing a tolerance
much more rapidly.

Another oral diabetic, anti-diabetic agent is the class of agents
called SGL-2 inhibitors. These include Invokana and are agents that cause you to release glucose
into the urine at a relatively low blood sugar. So instead of the renal threshold for glucose being,
let's say, 160 mg/dl, you may start peeing away glucose at a blood sugar of 130
or 120 or 110 mg/dl, thereby eliminating sugar from your bloodstream via the
urine. 

According to the literature, the major potential adverse effect of this would be urinary
tract infections. I have one or two patients who find this extremely effective, like it very much,
but there is a catch.

You have to drink a lot of fluid because you're going to be peeing away
a lot of fluid. You also have to urinate a lot. And if you're not drinking a lot of fluid, you
can get dehydrated. And on the few patients that I've used this for, I have seen signs in their
blood tests like a high hematocrit, that is high red blood cell concentration indicates that you
have too little water in your blood.

Or we'll see abnormalities in serum sodium level. So it is
possible to get potential problems if you get dehydrated or even if you drink too much fluid,
you could be peeing away a lot of sodium. So there are potential problems. The doctor should
be ordering blood tests every few months for sodium chloride, hematocrit, just to make sure
that things are running normal.

And as I said, some of my patients have found these to be
extremely valuable and therefore I can't say I won't use them. I'm just very reluctant to use
them because it means more work for me. I have to keep on top of those patients and retest their
bloods periodically. And that's basically the story with that group of drugs. SGLT2 inhibitors.

Last on the list is metformin, which for wise reasons the American Diabetes Association has
selected as their number one oral agent, the thing that you try first. And I agree with that,
but there are two categories of side effects. First category is GI disturbance, diarrhea usually,
and for that reason it should be started slowly and started with the time release version,
not the rapid acting version. And the time release should be taken in the lowest possible dose,
500 milligrams at the end of a meal to minimize the likelihood of diarrhea.

And then if you don't
get diarrhea, you can add to the dose, taking it at another time of the day. And you can eventually,
if so desired, switch from the slow release metformin to the rapid release if you want
more rapid action before meals.

The other side effect is inhibition of vitamin B12 absorption.
Metformin has most of its effects in the intestines and one of its adverse effects is the potential
for inhibiting vitamin B12 absorption. And this should not be ignored. I have not seen this happen,
but it's been repeatedly reported in the literature. And the articles that I read say
that if you consume calcium at the time that you take the metformin pill, you will eliminate that
side effect. Well, that doesn't mean that you take a Tums whenever you take a metformin tablet.

It means that your doctor first checks your B12 levels before starting the metformin.
And because certain B12 levels vary from day to day widely, depending upon what you ate,
you want to also look at homocysteine and methylmalonic acid levels because they go up
when B12 is low. So you get a baseline and then after a month on metformin, you see what happens
to B12, homocysteine, methylmalonic acid, and you see whether things are changing. And if they are,
then you tell the patient take a Tums with each metformin tablet and then you see what happens
by measuring blood levels.

As a matter of routine, once I've got a person rolling on metformin
without any adverse B12 absorption effects, I'll recheck maybe these three tests in six months or
a year and I'll do it again in another year or so just to make sure B12 absorption is not being
impaired. And one other warning is that we found that the generic metformin is much less effective
on blood sugar than the brand name Glucophage. And I've repeated that over and over in my various
communications.

### Does excess injected insulin raise cholesterol?
Well, first of all, why are you worrying about cholesterol? If you're diabetic, you should be
worrying about blood sugar. The cholesterol myth has pretty much been abandoned by the bulk of the
medical profession. If anything, there has been higher mortality in some groups with
very low cholesterol levels, but total cholesterol is no longer a risk factor for heart disease.

We do look at a related measure called oxidized LDL or glycated LDL, both of which can be affected
by high blood sugars. So I would say don't worry about cholesterol. And injected insulin is
a replacement for missing insulin. And one could say, well, all humans have a high cholesterol
because they're making insulin. Well, insulin doesn't do that anyway. So it's something you
don't worry about. I just think that it's a subject that has no meaning.

Large amounts of injected insulin injected to cover excessive intake of carbohydrate,
as advocated by the American Diabetes Association, will cause obesity. And the obesity
will cause all kinds of problems, including cardiac disease. So the game plan is not to
eat a lot of carbohydrates, so you don't have to inject excessive amounts of insulin.

### Pressure points on feet
*I have pressure points on bottom of feet, which are painful when walking.
Doctor recommended new shoes before he would recommend orthotics. Besides moisturizing with
oil, what else would you recommend to stop the pain until the shoes and orthotics start to work
to heal the pressure point?* 

Well, first of all, if the pressure point is a wound,
like a callus with an ulcer under it, that has to be attended to by a wound care
clinic or a wound care specialist. If the pain is just from the pressure from the shoes,
the game plan should be to immediately relieve it, not to experiment and wait.

What I would do when I was doing this kind of thing in clinic, and I still do it in my office,
is I will take out of my inventory what's called the cast boot, which is a removable
shoe that comes in a variety of sizes. I take one, the patient size, and I would glue to the
sole of the cast boot some cushioning material, it might be leather, it might be plastic,
to take the pressure off the site that's hurting. That's the short-term game plan.

So you could go to a podiatrist who could do that immediately in his office,
and if he can't do that, he's not the podiatrist you want to see. Then you go to
a shoe store with a replica of the little pad that is sitting in the cast boot,
and you try on a variety of shoes but have the pad stuck in the shoe temporarily. You could
use double stick tape to make sure that the shoe fits with the insert in place.
Then you take the new shoes back to the podiatrist and have him glue in an insert
that matches the one in the cast boot. 

Now, sometimes just a standard arch support will do
this. Sometimes you have to glue this post, as it's called, the extra piece of material,
it's called posting. You have to glue that underneath the arch support.
There are lots of ways that this can be done, but it should be done by a professional person,
and the pain should be stopped immediately if it's caused by the pressure. If it's caused by
