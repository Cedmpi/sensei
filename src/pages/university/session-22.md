Hi, this is Dr. Bernstein with another session of Dr. Bernstein's Diabetes University.
Today, I interrupted my usual sequence of sessions because something came up in the
scientific literature that is very relevant to people with diabetes. It appeared in the
December 13th edition of the journal Neuron and was reported by a group of researchers at the
University of Southern California. 

The title of the article is, Brains Protective Barrier
Disintegrates as People Age. Now, this sounds sort of very scientific, but it's pretty easy
to understand. These people point out that the blood-brain barrier, the interface between the
bloodstream and the brain, which protects the brain from toxic substances, is made up of cells
called pericytes. You may have heard me mention this term in an earlier session where we were
talking about complications of diabetes and insulin receptors and so on.

The pericytes are
the outer cells of capillaries, small blood vessels, and they're like an exoskeleton. They're
the outside skeleton of the capillaries and protect them from leakage. And the capillaries
in the brain that are supplying blood to the brain are selective in that they prevent toxic
substances from coming in. Now, here this study shows that the pericytes one by one disappear or
become leaky as people age. 

What they neglect to mention, probably because they don't realize it,
is that pericytes are what I called before aldose reductase cells. These are cells that will allow
the entry of glucose even without the presence of insulin. Glucose will diffuse freely into these
cells where it gets converted by an enzyme called aldose reductase into sorbitol. Sorbitol is an
osmotic substance that attracts water.

The principal ingredient in X-lax, the laxative,
is sorbitol because it will draw water into the intestines and loosen the stool. Well, aldose
reductase cells allow glucose to come in. The glucose gets converted to sorbitol, draws in
water, and if nothing is done to reduce the glucose in the bloodstream near that cell,
within two weeks it gets converted to fructose.

But if blood sugars remain high and more sorbitol
keeps coming in, so much water will be attracted into the cell that it'll swell up and eventually
burst. So this is a good way of killing pericytes, just have a high enough blood sugar for a long
enough period of time. 

Now you can actually see this parasite loss if you're a physician with
the help of an ophthalmoscope which looks at the retina of the eye and you will see pericyte loss
in the form of little red dots called microaneurysms. When a pericyte is lost, remember it's microscopic
in size, the underlying endothelial part of the vessel is no longer protected and it starts to
stretch and blow up like a balloon. And it blows up so much that it eventually becomes visible as
a little dot with the ophthalmoscope.

And many of you have heard the ophthalmologists tell you,
you have only a few microaneurysms. These represent loss of pericytes and you get these
same little things in the brain in particular around the hippocampus which is the part of the
brain that's responsible for processing short-term memory, memory of recent events.

So here we have a
lot of pericyte leakage visualized with I believe MRI scans and they show you pictures in the article,
color pictures of this leakage and it's occurring around the hippocampus and explains the memory
loss that we see in people with Alzheimer's disease.

Now you've heard from prior sessions
of Diabetes University that people with elevated blood sugars develop impairment of memory, IQ,
etc. And this we now know is one of the mechanisms for this impairment, namely high blood
sugars, aldose reductase pericytes that in effect balloon and blow up and eventually disappear
causing leakage of material into and out of the brain. Well I hope you enjoyed this. Stay tuned
