title: 50. Orthostatic hypotension
---
This is Dr. Bernstein of Dr. Bernstein's Diabetes University. Our subject for today is orthostatic hypotension.
A report recently came out in the New England Journal of Medicine in the May 2016 issue.
It's volume 64 of a study of people who are falling due to unknown causes, usually elderly people.
And what the study found was 40% of the supposed unknown causes of falling were orthostatic hypotension. That is blood pressure that drops when you stand up.

Now this is very common amongst diabetics because they tend to, people with high blood sugars, tend to get autonomic neuropathy. And one of the autonomic neuropathies is sympathetic neuropathy of the lower extremities.
This means that when you stand up, the muscles around the arteries in your legs that are supposed to squeeze the arteries closed so blood doesn't pool in your legs and deprive your brain of blood, these arteries aren't working.
The muscles aren't working because the nerves to the muscles aren't working. So a diabetic with autonomic neuropathy can stand up and pass out. His blood pressure drops dramatically.

Now it's interesting that when I was a medical student, I rotated on the medical wards and the most common admission in our population, which was an elderly population, was for people passing out.
And nowhere in the protocol was there to check their blood pressure supine and standing. We were to get electrocardiographs, get exercise stress tests, electroencephalograms.
They had a whole protocol that would take several days, very expensive, and none of these things were positive. Case after case, they didn't find anything.

Well, my patients, I would check their blood pressure supine and standing, and I'd always find they had orthostatic hypotension.
When they stood up, not only did their blood pressure drop, but they complained of feeling dizzy, and some of them had to sit down right away. So in my day, doctors were just not checking for this. And when I was doing it, I was ridiculed by the attending physicians.
The same thing happened when I went into my internship, but I wasn't ridiculed as much. The other interns started copying me.

So this is just a reminder that if you have orthostatic hypotension, when you get up at night to pee or for any other reason, you sit at the edge of your bed and dangle your feet for about a minute and get up very slowly.
Because you might fall over. And then if you're taking insulin, people will think you have hypoglycemia, and they'll give you glucose and not realize that it was due to orthostatic hypotension.

Very important, in extreme cases, we'll have people wear surgical stockings, maybe 20 to 30 millimeter compression, even when they're lying in bed. And in very extreme cases, people have to wear surgical pantyhose going all the way up to their abdomen.
It's very hard to get on and off and hard to pee and so on. But that those are extreme cases, but these cases do exist. So it's a very important subject because it's very common. It also occurs in elderly people who are not diabetic.
