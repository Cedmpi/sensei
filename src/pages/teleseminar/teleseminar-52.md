### Special subject: Comparing sulfonylureas glimepiride with DPP-4 linagliptin

This is a comparison of a [sulfonylurea glimepiride to a DPP-4 antagonist called liragliptin](https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article-abstract/42/12/2204/36196/Using-Real-World-Data-to-Predict-Findings-of-an).
Now the DPP-4 antagonists are used for type 2 diabetics as are the sulfonylureas, but the DPP4-s have very
little effect on blood sugar. They're almost useless.

The sulfonylureas, while they're acting, have a very strong effect on blood sugar if there are beta
cells still remaining. If there are no more beta cells, then the effect is minimal.

So you're comparing two things that are totally different and, in this case, they're comparing them
for cardiovascular harm because for years there were studies of sulfonylureas that claimed that they
caused cardiovascular disease.

There were good biochemical reasons for suspecting this, but this latest study, which was a large
one, looked at cardiovascular disease after an extended period of use of the lizagliptin versus the
glimepiride sulfonylurea, and here they're reporting that there's no cardiovascular harm, but they
totally ignored the fact that the sulfonylureas forced the pancreas to make more insulin, thereby
burning out beta cells.

It's been known for many years that the sulfonylureas are good for maybe 1 to 3 years, during
which time they cause beta cell burnout.

So why on earth would you want to give a sulfonylurea to anyone? Why do you look for cardiovascular
harm when you shouldn't even be using the product? Well, if you look at the authors' sources of
income, you'll see why.

That's a big mistake.

What this person has just told me in effect is that he or she wants to continue to follow ADA
