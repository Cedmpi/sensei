### Special subject: Nasal glucagon

Before we answer questions, we have two special subjects. The first one relates to the recent
announcement by Eli Lilly and Company of a nasal-administrated glucagon.

Now, as far as I can tell from their advertising and from press releases, they're advocating that it
be used by diabetics to raise blood sugar if their blood sugar is too low.

This is only a half-truth. It should only be used if the patient is unconscious and incapable of
consuming something by mouth voluntarily.

If the patient is semi-conscious, it probably would be a valid application also, but for someone who
is cognitive and can do what you ask him to do, he should be drinking liquid glucose or if he
doesn't have that available, chewing a glucose tablet to administer glucagon to someone who does not
really require it, brings with it some problems.

Glucagon works by telling the liver to convert the body's stored glycogen, which is a starch, back
to glucose, thereby raising the blood sugar.

However, once you've administered glucagon, you've used up much of the body's stored glycogen. So if
another hypoglycemic episode occurs later on, hours later or days later, I shouldn't say days, but
hours later, there may not be enough stored glycogen to make another dose of glucagon effective.

So that's one hazard.

The other hazard is a fairly significant incidence of nausea from the administration of glucagon.
Now the provider information says that nausea is a side effect but very rare. In my experience, it
has not been very rare. It is a significant percentage of the applications of glucagon for
hypoglycemia.

So this is a fine idea if you have an unconscious patient. You could literally squirt this stuff in
his nose. You don't even have to tell him to inhale because if he's still breathing, it'll get there
and it takes a while to work.

According to the manufacturer's literature, however, it takes as long as oral glucose would take.

So if the patient is conscious and can drink some liquid glucose, that certainly is the preferred
route. And of course, liquid glucose is far, far cheaper than glucagon.

### Special subject: Ketogenic diets and hypothyroidism

Our other special subject relates to a [study of epileptic children](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28076316/)
who are being treated with extreme ketogenic diets where they have to have their blood ketones continually grossly elevated.

This recent study shows that 17% of children, I believe ages 4 through 7 years develop
hypothyroidism after one year of a ketogenic diet. Now the study has not been extended yet to two
years, so it could be that even more kids will develop hypothyroidism after two years on a ketogenic
diet.

I've been for years advocating against ketogenic diets for the treatment of diabetes usually because
they deprive kids of protein or adults of adequate protein, but yet this is another reason why you
should not be following ketogenic diets and certainly not for children.

###  I'm a healthy 77-year-old high-risk heart patient with type 2 diabetes having an A1C of 7.6%, which is an average blood sugar of about 200 mg/dl. Is the evidence compelling to include Jardiance, which is an SGLT2 inhibitor in my all-insulin diabetic medication regimen to reduce chances of cardiovascular incidence? [short: Using SGLT2 inhibitor to reduce cardiovascular risk?]

Well, this is a wise question because there's been a lot of advertising and also promotion by the
American Diabetes Association that these SGLT2 inhibitors protect you against heart attacks.

My recollection, which may not be exact, is that they may reduce the incidence by about 20%, which
is not a huge reduction in incidence, but I also warn people that these SGLT2 inhibitors may have
other adverse reactions, and as I announced last month, they've been found to grossly increase the
incidence of amputations in diabetics. No reason given, but this has been an observation.

There are problems with the SGLT2 inhibitors, and it doesn't matter whether you're a type 1 or a
type 2 diabetic, I am not recommending them.

If you want to reduce your chances of a heart attack, work on your blood sugars, which are the usual
cause of heart disease and diabetics, and there are many things that you can do to normalize your
blood sugar.

Most of them are listed in my book, Diabetes Solution, so I'd say work on blood sugars and take care
of those first. Don't take chances with SGLT2 inhibitors for a 20% reduction.
