### Special subject: Preserving C-peptide production

For a special subject today,
we're going to talk about preserving C-peptide production.

The reason I brought up this subject
is because we get many questions from listeners.
How can I preserve my C-peptide level?

Now, what is C-peptide level?  
The precursor of insulin is a molecule called proinsulin.
Proinsulin has an extra short chain of amino acids attached to it
that gets clipped off when it gets converted to insulin.
This short chain is C-shaped on the proinsulin molecule.
So it's called C-peptide.

Now, for every insulin molecule that the body makes,
it has to therefore make one C-peptide molecule.

And it's more reliable to measure a person's C-peptide level
than their insulin level because if you're trying to look at endogenous insulin,
the insulin that a patient made, he could be injecting insulin,
raising his blood insulin level,
and totally confusing the insulin level,
a mixture of injected plus his own.

But if you look at C-peptide,
you'll just get a measure of his own insulin production.
So you can't deceive the C-peptide level.

By the way, most physicians specify a fasting C-peptide level.
I always prefer a post-meal C-peptide level with the blood sugar at the same time
because if a non-diabetic happens to have a low blood sugar,
or even a diabetic, they're not going to be making much insulin.

Their C-peptide will be low.
And if you eat a meal and raise your blood sugar,
the C-peptide should go up.
And if you have a high blood sugar and a low C-peptide,
you know this person is diabetic.

Anyway, we'll get on to the importance of C-peptide
over and above being an indicator that you're making insulin.

There's an article in a <a href="https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/27/6/1506/22660/Diabetes-Complications" class="underline" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">
2004 issue of the journal Diabetes Care</a>
that lists some of the functions of C-peptide by itself.
In other words, when insulin is made, C-peptide is made,
and the C-peptide does a lot of things that are important.

And I'll quote.  
*In patients and animal models,
C-peptide corrects glomerular hyperfiltration at the onset of diabetes.*
That means over-action of the kidneys, *reduces albuminuria,
albumin and urine, decreases mesangial matrix expansion.*
That's a part of the kidney that enlarges in diabetics
and compresses capillaries in the kidneys.

*C-peptide also normalizes nerve conduction velocity
in the early phase of diabetic neuropathy
and ameliorates or reverses diabetes-induced nerve structural changes.*

*C-peptide administration,* which is done, I guess, in animals,
*increases blood flow in peripheral nerve, kidney, skeletal muscle,
myocardium,* that's the cardiac muscle, *and skin,
accelerates myocardial repolarization,
increases ventricular contraction and relaxation velocities,
improves stroke volume, improves red cell deformability,
and increases muscle glucose uptake.*

So there are many, there's much value to C-peptide production
aside from the fact that it indicates that you're making insulin.

Now, as I've said over and over,
one of the best ways of preserving insulin production in early diabetics
is normal blood sugars,
because high glucose is toxic to beta cells that make insulin.

But there was another
<a href="https://diabetesjournals.org/care/article/36/7/1842/32976/Nutritional-Factors-and-Preservation-of-C-Peptide" class="underline" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"> article in Diabetes Care in the year 2013 </a>
that demonstrated that fish oil or omega-3 fatty acids
can improve the C-peptide, can preserve C-peptide in youth
with recently diagnosed type 1 diabetes.
So I assume that this would also apply to adults
with recently diagnosed type 2 diabetes or type 1.

So they don't make a recommendation as to how much omega-3 fatty acids
you should take, but if you eat oily fish on a regular basis,
if this study is correct, it should help preserve the C-peptide.

Will it be as effective as keeping blood sugars normal?  
My guess is no, that you have to do both,
and that the most important thing is to have normal blood sugars.

Now we'll get on to our questions.

### Does cooking the vegetables turn their slow-acting carbs into fast-acting?

It can. Depends how long.

If you cook the vegetables just long enough to warm them up,
it shouldn't make a difference.

But if you cook for hours, you're going to break bonds between glucose molecules.

And fiber, vegetables are high in fiber.
Fiber is polymers of glucose molecules, chains of hundreds or thousands of glucose molecules.
And they're broken apart by heat.
So the longer you cook them, the more likely you're going to turn fiber into rapid-acting carbohydrate.
