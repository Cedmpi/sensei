Today we're going to talk about a complication of diabetes called Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis.
Now this is one of the most common complications of diabetes.
Yet most doctors don't know about it.

They are taught in medical school about a condition called medial calcification.
And they're never taught about what causes it.
Now there was a recent article in one of the medical journals that found several explanations
for why this happens.
And I'll give you my old explanation and tell you a little about the condition.

When I was a kid, 12 years old, whenever I saw my doctor, he did very little in the way
of physical exam, even though he specialized in diabetes, but he would take a machine called
an oscillometer and measure something in my legs.

I'm going to show you an oscillometer in a second and I'll explain it to you.
This is the oscillometer.
As when I hold down this little lever, you can see the needle starting to swing and it
gives a little swing with each heartbeat.
When I let go of the lever, it stops swinging.
When I push it down, it starts to swing.
The other half of the system is a blood pressure cuff, which is unwrapping from my leg.
I'll show it to you.
Here's the blood pressure cuff.
Here's the bulb I used to pump it up.

The principle of oscillometry is now used in many of the automatic blood pressure machines
that are on the market and many devices that are sold as apps for cell phones, for measuring
blood pressure in your fingers, and so on and so forth.
What I look at this device is not blood pressure, which it can measure, but rather
the distance that the needle swings.
That distance that the needle swings measures two things simultaneously and it's not necessarily
easy to distinguish them.

One is, are the major arteries in the leg constricted or impaired?
Is the flow impaired?
In which case you'll get a very low swing.
The needle may only swing a quarter of a unit.
A unit is the number of lines that you saw on the dial.
Each line is one oscillometric unit.
So if someone has severe impairment of blood flow, the needle might only move a half a
unit or a quarter of a unit.

On the other hand, most diabetics, especially after the first few years with diabetes, have
wild swings.
They might swing by 15 or 30 of these units because the muscle in the wall of the arteries
has become calcified.
Now how does that happen?
The explanation that I gave in an article that was published in 2001 goes something
like this.

The muscular wall of the arteries in the legs is supplied by sympathetic nervous impulses
to close down whenever you stand up so that blood doesn't pool in your legs and deprive
your brains.
You would pass out if the blood pooled in your legs.
So that's a self-protective measure that is part of human evolution.
When you stand up, your arteries in your legs close down.

Now in diabetics, something else happens.
The arteries get calcified.
Not the lumen.
The hole is the lumen.
The muscular wall that goes around.
This is the lumen on the inside.
So we see in diabetics the muscular wall becoming calcified.

Now if the sympathetic nerves that activate that muscular wall die because of diabetic
neuropathy, the muscles are no longer innervated and as you know from polio, when muscles lose
their innervation, the muscles die.
Now in polio, when the muscles die, they disappear and the leg gets the appearance of a stick
because all the muscle around the bone has gone.

But in Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis, for reasons that are not clear to me but were recently
explained in one of the journal articles, the muscle turns to calcium.
It turns to stone.
The muscles become calcified.
And when you try to compress the artery with a blood pressure cuff, you get what's called
a bounding pulse and the needle swings a great distance due to this calcification.
And this calcification, also called medial calcification, is also called Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis.

Why is it called medial?
Because the arterial wall has three components.
It has an intima, the inside lining.
It has a tunica externa, which is an outer lining.
And then in the middle is the muscle, which is also called the tunica media.
And that's what calcifies.
So it's medial calcification.

Now, when I was in medical school, I had been diabetic, I don't know, let's say 30 years.
No, it must have been more than that.
I was, let's say, 48 years old, minus 12, I was diabetic 36 years.
And I decided to apply for the Jocelyn Foundation's 25-year medal.
And to pass the 25-year medal test, you have to show no Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis.
So they asked me to send an x-ray of my ankle and lower leg.
And I had this done at one of the hospitals that I was rotating through as a medical student.
And what we saw on the x-ray, the radiologist called trolley tracks, parallel lines, which
is what the view of a calcified artery would look like.
The calcium is thicker when you look through the sides of the artery than it will be in
the middle of the image.
So we saw these trolley tracks going up my legs, and we sent them to the people at the
Jocelyn Clinic, and the vascular people said, you fail, you have Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis.
And he was correct.

Now the people who qualify for the 25-year medal are extremely rare, because almost no
diabetics get away without getting Mönckeberg's, and that's the reason I'm talking about it
because it's so utterly common, universal.
And when I do this test on a brand new patient, I might get normal readings, and frequently
do.

But once they've had diabetes for over five years, when they first come to see me, they
clearly have these big wild swings.
Now if I see a patient at the time of diagnosis get his blood sugars normal and test him five
years later, we don't see Mönckeberg's.
We see a normal set of readings on the oscillometer.

Now one little bit of information.
My chief, the guy who ran the clinic where I worked for 30 years or so, was the world's
authority on the diabetic foot and on vascular disease and diabetics.
And he taught me that this condition was incurable.
And he measured the oscillometry on me when we first met, which was in 1982 or 1983, I
think.
And I had a reading of 18 in the calf and it was 12 in the ankle.
The angle is four to 11 in the calf and half that in the ankle.
So it would be two to five and a half in the ankle.
So I was grossly abnormal.
I really was loaded with calcium in my arteries.

About five years ago, I was lecturing the residents in my clinic and we had run out
of patients.
It was always easy to demonstrate Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis with the oscillometer in diabetics
because they all had it.
And all the people who ended up in our clinic were long-term diabetics.
So I said, well, we have no patients.
I'll be the victim and I'll let one of you guys measure the oscillometry on me.
And I showed them how to do it.
I was 8 in the calf and 4 in the ankle.
And that's sort of like in the middle of the normal range.
Not only that, the ratio of the calf to the ankle should be two to one.

If you have Mönckeberg's, there's a lower ratio.
So here, some 30 years after I had originally been tested, the Mönckeberg's had gone away.
Well, I was the only victim probably in the world who had experienced this reversal of
Mönckeberg's.

I'm not even going to attempt to publish it because, number one, in medicine, you usually
don't publish just one case.
They do it in psychiatry but usually not in the medical journals.
So people want verification from additional cases.
And secondly, the author was Richard K. Bernstein, who finds it very hard to get published in
the official American Diabetes journals because he's been considered an opponent of the standard
treatment of diabetes.

So here, after 30 odd years, we reversed my Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis.
Why did it reverse?
Well, we do know that neuropathies reverse when blood sugars are normalized.
We know that the longer the nerves, the longer it takes to reverse the neuropathy.
Now, I could speculate on what happened with me.
I had normal blood sugars for 35 years or there or whatever it was.
It was actually since 1972.
And so for these many years, I had normal blood sugars, probably regrew the sympathetic
nerves.
There may have been a little bit of muscle, of smooth muscle left in my, in the arteries
in my legs.
And for all these years, the sympathetic nerves are now stimulating this little bit of muscle
and apparently, the muscle must have regrown under the influence of new nervous stimulation.
What happened to the calcium?
I don't know.

But new muscle, smooth muscle came about thanks to normal blood sugars.
And I can assure you that there's nothing else that could reverse this other than normal
blood sugars, unless they develop some new hormones or something that cause nerves to
regrow.
So you keep blood sugars normal, you could prevent Mönckeberg's and I've prevented Mönckeberg's
in many of my patients.
It's harder to reverse it.
But that certainly is possible.
In any event, this is a virtually universal condition.
Any doctor can test for it if he has an oscillometer.
There's a problem though.
In the USA, you can no longer buy oscillometers, where many years ago, every doctor would have
one in his office.

They're not even available because doctors are no longer taught hands on care in medical
school.
So there are one or two oscillometers, electronic oscillometers available.
I believe they're made in New Mexico, that are used for research studies.
And they're quite costly, so that doctors, number one, don't even know they exist, but
they also wouldn't want to spend the money for them.
So that's the story.
It's a very interesting condition.

There's still one problem that's out there in the medical industry, and that is how to
distinguish coronary artery calcification from Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis.
When you do a calcium score of the coronary arteries, where you take a picture that displays
the amount of calcium in the walls of the coronary arteries, how do you know that you're
not looking at Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis, rather than calcium deposits in the interior,
in the lumen of the artery?

So deposits that adversely affect the flow of blood.
I don't know how that can be done.
I've asked radiologists how it can be done, and they can't answer it for me.
We used to think that Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis had no adverse effects, but we now know that
it does.

And in <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11473102/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" class="underline">my article</a>, I point out a
recent study that occurred just after I submitted the article, that the smooth muscle cells
in the blood vessels, in the arteries, produce a substance that stabilizes plaque in the
coronary arteries.

So if you lose your smooth muscle cells, if they become calcified, they're not going to
produce the stabilizer, and plaque that protrudes into the lumen of the artery is more likely
to break loose, move upstream in the coronary arteries, and block something and cause a
heart attack.

Now that plaque that can break loose is called soft plaque.
It can be imaged by a CT angiogram, but once you have Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis, that
soft plaque is more likely to break off.
So the radiologists find that to predict heart attacks, it's a good idea to get both the
coronary artery calcium score, which might or might not be mostly Mönckeberg's atherosclerosis,
plus the CT angiogram, two tests.

And these two tests are frequently performed in conjunction at radiology centers around,
probably around the world.
They're now a pair of tests that jointly predict future heart attacks.
It's an interesting subject.
