<small> Discussion between *Dr. RD Dikeman (child has Type 1 diabetes)* and Dr. Bernstein </small>

*Now we're going to talk a little bit about insulin
management.
So you do something to manage your meal plan that most people are not familiar with.
Most people when they're diagnosed are given rapid acting insulin and huge amounts of carbohydrate
food suggestions from their dieticians.
And so when they read your book or they talk to someone following your plan, they've never
even heard of what we call an R or regular insulin.
So can you talk about the importance of using R in your meal plan and your plan to get normal
blood sugars?*

Okay, type 1 diabetics for the most part at least in the USA are using basal bolus insulin
regimens.
This means that they have a long acting insulin that they take twice a day to cover the fasting
state.
If they're not eating, they still need a little bit of insulin all day long and that's the
basal insulin.
To cover the glucose that comes in from meals, they need rapid acting insulin and there are
several different classes of rapid acting insulin with different action profiles.
The slowest of these is regular insulin which is true human insulin that's diluted 25 fold
and it starts typically for the average person, it starts working about 40 minutes after an
injection and continues working for 8 to 10 hours but most of its action is during the
first 4 or 5 hours.
Now if you're on a low carbohydrate diet, this is just the timing you need for your
insulin.

This is you're matching the digestion of the carbohydrate and protein that you're eating.
If you're on a very high carbohydrate diet as the professional diabetes associations
and endocrine associations recommend, then the logical thing is to take a faster acting
insulin.
The faster acting insulins are called Novalog and Humalog and there's another that's sort
of a little bit slower than those called Apidra.
Now in theory, these would work great but the trouble is that these insulins have a
sharp peak and high carbohydrate foods, rapid acting carbohydrates have a sharp peak and
you're expected to match those two peaks in time, a near impossibility.
Using the term near is even exaggerating because it's a true impossibility because the people
who do this never match.
They always go too high or too low after meals and then they go too high or too low later
on.

So it's impossible to make this kind of match where you're trying to take two peaks and
match them.
What we're doing with regular insulin, low carbohydrate and protein is taking a shallow
hill, two shallow hills like this and attempting to match them and if you're a little bit off,
you're not going to make a huge mistake.
So your timing may be a little off but if it's off today, tomorrow, you can adjust it
by five minutes or so and that might put you right on because you're just matching up two
shallow hills.


*It's a very physics-based argument you're making.
So even if these parents, I see a lot of discussion where a parent will be obsessed with a cupcake
if it has 42 or 45 or 41 grams of carbohydrate, if only they could get the carb count correct.
You're saying that carb counting doesn't work because of this analogy about these envelopes
overlapping.*

Right.
You're dealing with mystical things when you think that being precise on counting carbohydrate
is going to make up for the fact that you're trying to match a sharp peak with a sharp
peak from insulin.
It's not going to work.
It's impossible.
Here we have the insulin makers, the doctors, the dieticians all pretending that the impossible
not only is possible but it's working every day and that all these people with the rapidly
varying blood sugars are well controlled.

*So how do I use R if I'm lucky enough to convince my endocrinologist to give me a
prescription of R? By the way, R is available over the counter, right?*

In many states.

*I think it's like 20 bucks at Sam's Club.
But suppose my kid eats a couple pieces of chicken and his meal plan.
How do you use the R?*

Well, first of all, in his meal plan, he's not just getting a couple of pieces of chicken.
He's getting a weight amount or a reproducible amount that he's getting every day and he's
getting a reproducible amount of carbohydrate that's kept relatively low and we'll start
off with a guess as to how much regular to use for this meal.
Now, I have formulas that I use that are fine for people who don't have insulin resistance
and the formulas are simple.

One unit of R, in theory, covers eight grams of carbohydrate and a half unit of R
covers one ounce of protein (food).
So when you're starting your meal plan, your initial guess could be by doing the computations
that I just made and then maybe lowering it by 20% to play it safe, to be on the safe
side.

Chances are it will be too low but you see what happens to the blood sugars and then
you can increase the R the next day for those meals, a half unit per meal or a unit per
meal based upon how much that one unit will raise your blood sugar.
Well, how do you know that?
With a very obese individual who's insulin resistant or someone who's pregnant or a woman
who's having her period next week, it's really a guess as to how much one unit will lower
blood sugar.
For a person who's not obese, for a kid who's not in the middle of a growth spurt, someone
who weighs let's say 150 pounds (68 kg), one unit of insulin will lower them about 40.
That's an initial guess.

So here after this meal, your blood sugar is running 40 higher than you would like it
to be.
The next time for that meal, you add one unit to the insulin.
If you're running 20 higher, you add a half unit, et cetera.
So it's plain and simple.
The math is not complicated.
Doctors don't want to even learn this.
It's much easier to just take a lot of carbohydrate or fast-acting insulin and then close your
eyes and throw the patient out there to take care of himself or give him to a nurse educator
or a dietitian and let them worry about it.
If you've looked at dietitians, most dietitians are overweight.
Many of them are very obese and many of them are diabetic and those who are diabetic are
frequently taking large doses of insulin to cover all the carbohydrate they're eating.

*My experience with my kid, I'm going to advertise what you're saying is correct.
The experience is that we have his basals correct.
He wakes up in the morning with normal blood sugars right around 80.
He has his breakfast and he does his shot of R and he goes to school and now he'll test
before lunch maybe twice and his line is flat all day until lunch and then at lunch he does
another dose.
He eats a lunch.
He eats a breakfast and lunch that he loves so he's comfortable with the food and then
he's flat until he comes home.
He loves this because he wants to play at recess.  
He doesn't want to report to the nurse's office with a blood sugar of 400.  
He doesn't want to feel bad all day.  
He wants to live his life.  
The regimen that you're describing is it's not a set it and forget it.
He still has to do a finger prick and make sure but if he needs to correct himself he
might do a half a tab of glucose which he keeps in his pocket not sucking down juice
boxes in a severe state of hypoglycemia.
For some reason his blood sugar runs high, he takes a little bit of insulin and he's
back to normal.  
It's a totally different way of living your life.    
It's like our life has been restored as a family.*

It's unfortunate that neither the medical profession nor paramedical professionals nor

Outcomes like this are of no interest.
