### Why do some people with low hemoglobin A1Cs less than 5.3% get complications? [short: Low HbA1c  less complications]

That's a good question. I don't know where you got this information from, but it really depends on how long the A1C is elevated. 5.3 is not low relative to our ancestors. If we look at the people from whom we've evolved over the eons, people who had no agriculture, who had no sugars, could not get sweet foods, they probably were walking around with A1Cs between 4.2 and 4.6. And we do know from a number of studies that the effects of glucose on many systems of the body, including the brain, is a continuous one.

That is, a slightly high blood sugar has slight effect, adverse effect on many systems, and a higher blood sugar has a higher effect, etc. So 5.3% is a little above ideal, and one would slowly over many years be expected to develop diabetic complications. But people with 5.3% are going to have a much better time than those that have the 6.5 or 7% that the American Diabetes Association recommends.

The difference in average blood sugar between, say, 7% and 5.3, is 5.3 is an average blood sugar of about 112 and 7% is about 180. So that's quite a difference. By the way, I might add that several studies have shown that in non-diabetics, the lowest mortality is with an average blood sugar of around 83.

For example, if you're, this person's asking about boxing, if you're boxing over the course of an hour, even if you're non-diabetic, if you're really doing it strenuously, you're likely to use up all your stored glycogen, so you'd be wise to be consuming glucose beverages over the course of that hour. The protein is essential for building muscle. If you don't consume much more protein than a non-athlete would, you're not going to build muscle. So I would go by blood sugar, and I would say that for adults, an average blood sugar of 83 is good. If you're not diabetic and after strenuous activity go down to 70, that wouldn't do you any harm. But I wouldn't go by A1C. Not only that, different labs have different normals for A1C and even different values on the same individual. There are A1C test kits that the labs use, and one manufacturer's test kit might give different results than in others. So go by blood sugar rather than A1C.

### Thoughts on SGL2 inhibitors?

*Diagnosed with autoimmune Graves' disease in 2009 and type 2 diabetes. What are your thoughts on SGL2 inhibitors? I was in ICU with euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis from Invokana.*

Well, Invokana is a class of drugs called SGL2 inhibitors. These are things that cause you to pee away glucose. Well, they lower the renal threshold for glucose, which means that where a normal person or even a diabetic might start peeing substantial amounts of glucose in their urine with a blood sugar of 165 or 70, it might lower it to 120.

So that when your blood sugar is 120, you start peeing away a lot of glucose, thereby lowering your blood sugar. There are a host of potential adverse effects from this, because it can dehydrate you, can cause problems if you have kidney disease already. And the medical profession has erroneously called a syndrome that this causes that this can cause diabetic ketoacidosis.

Well, diabetic ketoacidosis is caused by severe dehydration compounded with very high blood sugars. Here, with this stuff, you get severe dehydration and ketoacidosis without high blood sugars. So it's not caused by diabetes. It's caused by the Invokana, which has caused severe dehydration. 

It's misnamed, and the whole medical profession and all the diabetes associations are calling it by the incorrect name. But it's caused by the Invokana. And this would happen in anybody, even if they were not diabetic, if they were greatly dehydrated. And the answer to that problem is that you have to consume a lot of fluid if you're taking Invokana.

And if you're taking Invokana and have high blood sugars to begin with, you have to consume huge amounts of fluid because you're going to be peeing away huge amounts of fluid and potentially getting dehydrated.

### Could you discuss the connection between anemia and A1C value? [short: Connection between anemia and A1C value]

Okay, I hope I get it straight. There are two kinds of anemia. There's macrocytic and microcytic. In microcytic anemia, your system is making new red blood cells at a rapid rate, but they're not of normal size.

And their lifespan is limited. In macrocytic anemia, usually due to not enough B12 or folate, the cells don't reproduce fast enough and they keep growing and therefore they're exposed to glucose for longer periods of time. And since when the hemoglobin is exposed to glucose, it gets glycated and you get glycated hemoglobin, which is hemoglobin A1C. So these older cells, when they're lysed to measure glycohemoglobin levels, will show a higher glycohemoglobin. So macrocytic anemia should show a higher glycohemoglobin level and microcytic anemia should show a lower level.

That's about all I can say right now, but there's probably a lot more to it because hematocrit may have an effect and so on. But I haven't boned up on this lately. I'm just going on what's in the back of my head.

### Using syringe on insulin pen cartridge?
*Insulin pen package inserts state that syringes should not be used with insulin pens. Is there any real reason not to use syringes with insulin pens?*

I'll explain what this person is talking about and this is a very wise question. Some of the insulin pens are provided with replacement cartridges of insulin so that you could either use the pen to deliver the insulin or if you want to go to the trouble, you can take an insulin syringe, stick the needle in the stopper of a syringe, a single cartridge that's not in the pen, and draw insulin out of the cartridge directly into an insulin syringe. 

And if you're using one of the syringes that has half unit markings, you could actually draw out a half unit or two and a half units and you can even estimate quarter units. You could get an estimate of three and a quarter units whereas with the pens, most pens will only go to the nearest whole unit and some pens will only deliver to the nearest two units. So you could get more accuracy.

Now you have to be careful of two things. The cartridges have a gasket at the opposite end from the stopper, from the diaphragm that you puncture and this gasket moves when you draw out insulin but there's a lot of friction. So you have to pull on the plunger of your syringe very slowly or that gasket won't move and you'll get air in your syringe. The air will come out the back of the syringe because you're creating a vacuum.

And you also must stick the needle in the very center of the diaphragm because the diaphragms tend to be very thick at the periphery. So you have to find the good eyes, you have to find the center of the diaphragm, puncture it there and very slowly draw out the insulin. 

And indeed this can give you more accuracy. Now if you're an obese type 2 diabetic and take huge doses of insulin, that degree of accuracy may not be important. But if you're a small person like me or a child, it makes a big difference.
