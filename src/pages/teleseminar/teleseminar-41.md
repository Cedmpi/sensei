### Special subject: Regular Insulin

One problem with regular, I should have included this as a special subject. Human insulin
can coagulate in the very fine needles used with most insulin pens. So if you have an insulin pen
and are using a 31-gauge needle, it could be that 10 hours later the insulin that's sitting in the
needle has totally coagulated and will not allow you to squirt out any more insulin so you have to
throw the needle away. 

If you're not checking your needle before each use, you might be injecting
nothing thinking you're getting a shot but your blood sugars are going to go up. If you're
injecting regular insulin for a meal and nothing's coming out, your blood sugars will go up. So be
very careful. This is a very important problem and must be dealt with continually. You don't know how
long that needle is going to last without being coagulated. 

Now, if you're taking a second shot
only 2 hours later, chances are it will not have coagulated. I usually don't recommend that in my
book but if you wait 5 or 6 hours, it might have coagulated. You've got to try to squirt a
couple of units out. You could squirt it on your hand. You could squirt it in a glass of water just
to see if any insulin is coming out. 

### Diabetic frozen shoulder

The first question deals with diabetic frozen shoulder, and I want to give you an explanation
of this in advance of the question. As far as I can tell, diabetic frozen shoulder is a result
of the glycation of collagen in the connective tissue near the shoulder. It doesn't just show
up near the shoulder, you can have carpal tunnel syndrome which can be caused from glycation of
collagen in the carpal band around the wrist. There is Dupuytren's contracture which has a
similar ideology. 

What happens is the fibers in this connective tissue get glued together
by the glucose. Glucose sticks to protein in the fibers, usually the protein is collagen.
Why collagen? Because collagen is probably the longest lived protein in the body. In a healthy
21 year old, I've read that collagen has a lifetime of 15 years.

Once you've glycated
collagen, it's around for a long time, and the fibers are glued together by the glucose. If you
try to move the joint that's encased in glued fibers, you might tear some of the fibers.
Ordinarily, they move relative to one another, but if you're trying to move them and they're
glued together, they might get torn apart causing inflammation. This inflammation is
what can cause the pain or restricted range of motion on what's called the frozen shoulder.
It usually doesn't freeze totally, but there's a sequence or progression of degrees of loss of
mobility. 

One way to tell if you have a diabetic frozen shoulder is to reach behind your back and
try to touch your spine. See how high up the spine your hand can go. You start with the dominant hand,
which is usually the first one to be affected, the dominant shoulder. You move your hand up,
and then you go with your non-dominant hand and see if the non-dominant hand goes higher
than the dominant. If it does, you know that the dominant one has limited range of motion.

I won't go into the treatment, which appears in an article that's on the website for my book,
Diabetes Solution, but we'll now go into the question. You'll see from what I just told you.

*If collagen breakdown in the connective tissues leads to frozen shoulder, could it also lead to eye floaters? Would taking a collagen supplement help?*

Well, as I just explained, it's not collagen
breakdown. It's the gluing of collagen fibers together by glucose. This question has no meaning
when you look at reality.

Certainly, taking a collagen supplement would have nothing to do,
would not stop the fibers from being glued together by glucose. Of course, this only
occurs when blood sugars are elevated. You don't see it ordinarily in non-diabetics or
in diabetics with normal blood sugars.

First of all,
what causes insulin resistance? We're going to answer that in another question. 

A low carb diet

your remaining beta cells telling them to make a lot of insulin.

They're making less insulin and
the antibodies to the beta cells in the pancreas are specifically to insulin made by the beta cells

If you're not making the
vesicles or you're making very little insulin, there's little of it to be attacked. Whereas,
if you're getting a lot of carbohydrate, you have to make a lot of insulin to cover it,
you're going to be attacked more often and more severely. If you're newly diagnosed and
are still making insulin, you certainly don't want a high carbohydrate diet. 

You're asking
what advice I would suggest that you get a family care doctor who will patiently listen
to logical arguments.

### What's your definition of the normal range of postprandial glucose? [short: Normal range of postprandial glucose?]

To me, it's the same as preprandial. Now how on earth did I come
up with that? I took a look at my guess of what our ancestors ate, and how much, and so on. Well,
we know from historical evidence, archaeological evidence, that before the advent of agriculture,
our ancestors ate almost exclusively living creatures, and therefore did not eat much in
the way of plants. They may have been able to scavenge a few edible roots or leaves,
there were not the sweet fruits that have been deliberately cultivated in modern times,

their blood sugars, given only protein, and usually not necessarily huge amounts,

Remember, they're non-diabetic. So my ideal

we have very little change, maybe in extreme cases, five or ten mg/dL.
So ideally, we're looking for no change after meals. 

### What causes insulin resistance?

Well,
first of all, there's mesenteric fat. That's fat that surrounds your intestines,
not the fat that you could grab on the outside of your belly. 

Elevated blood sugars cause insulin
resistance. So people who type 1 diabetics who have to inject insulin will require much more
insulin to get, let's say, a blood sugar of 650 down to 600 than they would to get a 150 down to 100.
So even though you're only dropping by 50 mg/dL, you need much more
insulin if your blood sugar is higher. 

Dehydration will lower your blood volume and lower the
delivery of blood glucose and insulin to your peripheral tissues. So dehydration acts as if
it's equal to insulin resistance, but it's more of a mechanical phenomenon causing insulin
resistance. 

Inflammation causes insulin resistance. So whether it's osteomyelitis in a jawbone or in
a toe, blood sugars will go sky high due to insulin resistance caused by the inflammation
which was caused by infection. 

Many medications will cause insulin resistance, such as steroids,
certain supplements. 

There's also scar tissue at injection sites. Now can you call that insulin
resistance? I found that most people who have come in here who are using insulin pumps are pumping in
a lot of insulin because it's not getting absorbed because they're pumping it into scar tissue. So
one can say that scar tissue causes insulin resistance. There are probably a number of
other causes of insulin resistance that I haven't even thought of.

### What's your opinion about the diagnosis of type 1.5 diabetes? [short: Opinion on type 1.5 diabetes? ]

Well, I've heard people talk about 1.5, 1.3, 1.25. This is, as far as I'm concerned,
it's a nonsense diagnosis. Their diabetes is a spectrum. It can be very mild and where you're
making a lot of insulin or very severe where you're making absolutely no insulin.

There are
people who are called type 1 diabetics, usually children who show high antibodies to insulin or
to their pancreas at some point in time, who are still making a lot of insulin and therefore are
not thought to be diabetic by many endocrinologists. So here you have a type 1 who's
very mild. You could have type 2s who have had type 2 diabetes for so long that their high blood
sugars have burned out their beta cells and they now require as much insulin as a type 1 diabetic.

There are all kinds of variations in there and to try to give absolute numbers to the disease
you have to me doesn't make sense. I say treat the blood sugar not the name and you do whatever
is necessary to treat the blood sugar to normalize the blood sugar around the clock.
