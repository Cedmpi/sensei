The subject for today's session will be why do some people object to the use of insulin?
I remember many years ago when I knew Dr. Robert Atkins who very much shared my belief
in low carbohydrate diets, but one of his reasons for the low carbohydrate diet was
not what was weight loss and he said that the reason people got fat was because carbohydrate
increased their insulin production and insulin can build fat.

In fact, indeed it does when you have a lot of carbohydrate in your diet or even if you
eat huge amount of protein which eventually gets converted to glucose if you're not eating
anything else, that glucose can be converted to fat.
So he had an objection to insulin on that score, but of course these excessive amounts
of insulin would be reduced by cutting the carbohydrate.

In my discussions with him it appeared that he rarely used insulin to treat his patients
and in those days my books were brand new, I should say my one book was brand new.
No one knew how to use insulin in small amounts to precisely control blood sugar so that everyone
who was using insulin was causing frequent episodes of hypoglycemia.

So there were problems associated with it in those days, yet even today many doctors
refuse to use insulin and many doctors give theoretical reasons to justify what usually
is their inability to use it, their lack of knowledge of the details of using small amounts
of insulin and if you read my book, Diabetes Solution, you can see that it's not a simple
matter to use insulin and it's a myth to assume that any physician really knows how
to use small amounts of insulin to precisely offset meals with small amounts of carbohydrate
and so on.

It requires a lot of details and a lot of knowledge.
So you can see that number one, most doctors don't even know how to use it and their use
of it by implication has to be unsafe and indeed many physicians recommend against the
use of insulin simply because of the hazard to themselves.

As I've said in these sessions before, doctors have told me that they don't believe in normal
blood sugars because their patients at the high blood sugars they advocate may develop
all the long-term complications of diabetes, congestive heart failure, blindness, kidney
failure, et cetera, amputations but if they keep their blood sugars high, they won't develop
hypoglycemia and if we look at what are the potential downsides of insulin, a major downside
is that if you don't know how to use it, you stand strong odds of causing hypoglycemia
and most patients and most doctors do not know how to use insulin.

So there's a lot of very low blood sugars out there amongst insulin-using patients.
So statistically you can say, yeah, they're all going to get hypoglycemia because they
don't know what they're doing and that's likely.

If you read my books, however, and know how to use insulin, it's another story altogether
and of course doctors will say, look, if I have one patient who dies from hypoglycemia,
I get sued.

The fact that the high blood sugars are causing multiple deaths and bizarre and painful complications
to all the rest of my patients is unimportant to me because if I get sued, I might be out
of business.

So that's a major reason for not using insulin.
As I mentioned before, there's also the fact that many doctors and patients follow the
ADA philosophy, eat a lot of carbohydrate, inject a lot of insulin to cover the carbohydrate
so that you don't go into such high blood sugars that the high blood sugars kill you
right off the bat.

So here we have this advocacy of high carbohydrate and high insulin generating new insulins that
are highly concentrated, U300, U500, so that people can take smaller shots to cover the
large amounts of carbohydrate that they're consuming.
So this ability to turn carbohydrate into fat by injecting insulin is a disadvantage,
but if you don't eat the carbohydrate, you don't have that problem.

I'm trying to think, there are no basic physiologic disadvantages to normal glycemia and the amount
of insulin that you need to cause normal glycemia if you don't overeat carbohydrate.
So if you're on a low carbohydrate diet, getting small amounts of insulin, which is needed
to cover both carbo and other food sources like protein, there's no physiologic harm
to the insulin.

So here we have, insulin can be an inconvenience, it can make you fat if you eat a lot of carbohydrate,
and it can cause hypoglycemia if you're not knowledgeable about how to use it.
Those are the three disadvantages to insulin.

The advantages are that for many people, in my experience, most type 2 diabetics, you
cannot get normal blood sugars without it.
Maybe I have five type 2 patients, probably less, maybe three, who are only getting oral
agents to treat their blood sugars, and they're keeping normal.
That's because they're making enough of their own insulin to fill in what the oral agents
don't do for them.

But all the rest of my type 2 diabetics get to a point where we're adding oral agents,
we're trying some that don't work, some that are causing adverse effects, some that have
other hazards, and maybe we end up with one oral agent or two, and the blood sugars are
still not normal, they're not walking around with blood sugars around 83, or A1c is between
4.2 and 4.6.

So we add insulin, and the little bit of insulin that we add doesn't do any harm, but I can
understand why doctors who do not know how to use insulin are afraid of it.
And I might add further that many of my patients are using diluted insulins.
If you were to hear me talking on the phone to a patient of mine at some distant point,
might be another country, might be another state, and we're talking about RD or HD, we're
talking about diluted, the D stands for diluted.
And each patient who needs diluted insulin gets their own ratio of dilution that is most
convenient for them.

So when you deal with diluted insulin, you're dealing with very small amounts, we're not
dealing with the big doses that are advocated by the ADA to cover large amounts of carbo.
I hope this clarifies the issue.
It's not an easy one to explain, but good luck.
