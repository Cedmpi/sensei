### Special subject: Diabetes complications despite A1C below 7

Before we start answering questions, I was asked to speak on a special subject.

I was asked to talk about my patients who did not have hemoglobin A1Cs under 5 and therefore got
diabetic complications.

The trouble is that the patients whom I am treating have A1Cs about 5 or under 5, but I see many new
patients, patients who are new to me. I did not diagnose their diabetes, but they've had it a number
of years and they'll come in with an A1C of 6, 6.57, and I'll do a physical exam.

My physical exam is really 10 pages long, 8 of the pages deal with just complications of diabetes,
and on average for these people, I find 15 to 20 different complications of diabetes.

They're rarely blind or unable to walk when they come here because they usually have to travel great
distances.

They have to be able to read my book because I won't treat anyone who hasn't read my book Diabetes
Solution, but these people who have A1Cs of maybe 6, 6.5, going by the ADA guidelines perhaps for
years and years, always have many complications.

Every chart that I have of a patient that I'm treating starts off with a load of diabetic
complications.

Many of them get better when we have normal blood sugars, so things like double vision, which the
patients usually don't even know they have, but can be elucidated if you look through a red lens
with one eye and not with the other eye.

That afflicts most of the new patients, but after a few years of normal blood sugars, it reverses,
and many of the neuropathies reverse, pain in the feet and so on.

We can reverse many complications with normal blood sugars, cannot reverse all of them.

If someone is blind, you're not going to make them sightful again, so the game plan really should be
to have normal blood sugars. For adults, that means blood sugars around the clock of around 83 mg/dL and
for kids in the 60s and 70s.

Before we start with our questions, I want to remind you that the answers I give are generic
answers.

I cannot know the entire medical history of someone who asks a question. I can't do a physical exam
on him, so I guess at an answer based on my knowledge of other people. My answer for Mr. X may not
apply to Mr. Y and might not even apply to Mr. X, but might apply to the other guy who didn't ask a
question.

### This is from a 60 year old female. Recently I've had to up my Tresiba by 2 units because of rising blood sugars for no reason without eating anything for hours. Could this be insulin resistance suddenly showing up? [short: Sudden insulin resistance?]

Well, again, if you read my book, Diabetes Solution, it will point out a number of possibilities.
One possibility is infection and we even have a chapter on the possibilities of infections in the

Then another possibility is that you gained some weight and became more insulin resistant if you're

Then with Tresiba, which comes in pens, there's a big hazard of losing insulin where you think
you're getting a dose that you injected, but it's actually leaked out of you.

And for that reason, I tell every person who's using an insulin pen, actually I tell every patient
who's injecting insulin to wipe their hand over their skin at the site of injection after the
injection to see if it's wet.

I've been doing this since I was a child. Back when I was a child and was on a high carbohydrate
diet, I was injecting in one shot 200 units of insulin. We had U500 then, so it wasn't that big, but
it was a pretty big dose and it almost always leaked.

That was without a pen, and I also used to smell my hand because the insulin had a preservative in

You should do this sort of thing with any insulin injections, but especially if you're using a pen,
and if you're getting leakage, you have to figure out how to avoid it.

One way is to use longer needles. When I use an insulin pen, I use a 12.7 millimeter needle for all
my shots, not the 3 or 4 millimeter needle that comes with the pen.

I have many of my patients actually using an insulin syringe to take insulin out of a pen cartridge.
If you're clever, you could figure out how to do this.

When you press on the plunger of an insulin syringe, your thumb can be pretty powerful, but when you
push the button on a pen, you're not pushing out the insulin. You're releasing a spring that you
wound up when you turned the knob on the pen, and that spring is not very strong, and it might not
be able to fully eject all of the insulin in your dose deep enough.

It may be necessary for you actually to take an insulin syringe and go to the cartridge that's 
in the pen and draw your dose out of the cartridge while it's in the pen. 
There are other cartridges that are removable from pens.

Likewise, you could use them with an insulin syringe.
When drawing insulin out of a cartridge, it's not as easy as drawing insulin out of a vial or bottle because 
drawing the insulin out doesn't merely remove insulin. It removes the little stopper at the far end of the cartridge,
and there's a lot of friction between the stopper and the glass of the cartridge,
so you're going to have a lot of resistance to your pulling, and if you pull fast,
you're going to create a vacuum and draw air up the far end of the syringe. 

The back of the syringe is going to leak air because of this vacuum, so you have to pull very slowly
on the plunger of the syringe so that you will not get any air in your syringe.

### Can an A1C drop too quickly, compromising health in any way? Not to my knowledge except for one situation. My type 1 diabetic daughter went from a 5.1 to a 4.6 in two months. [short: Negative effects from dropping A1C too quickly?]

A1C, well I can think of two situations.

One is when you adapt to a certain blood sugar, if you suddenly have much lower blood sugars, you
