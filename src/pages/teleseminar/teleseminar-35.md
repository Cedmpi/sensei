### Special subject: Verapamil for type 1 diabetes prevention

There was an announcement in the [Journal Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-018-0089-4) about research conducted recently or
reported recently by the University of Alabama in Birmingham. They experimented with a hypertension
drug for Verapamil that's been around for probably more than 30 years, a pretty benign drug. They've
been experimenting with it for the prevention of type 1 diabetes.

And according to their report, in many of the patients who were tested, I say many, I think
something like 30 or 40 patients were tested and only about half of them were on the product, the
others received a placebo. So it was not many patients. And my recollection is that it may have only
been 13 patients who experienced a halt to the progression of new onset diabetes, type 1 diabetes.

So this treatment supposedly protects the beta cells from burnout. The word that they use is called
aptosis, which means self-destruction.

Now why do I take the trouble or the time to discuss this particular experiment when there are so
many other so-called cures being reported every month or two? The reason is because I've used
medications like this for other purposes and they are very benign. I've not seen adverse effects
from them.

This verapamil is in a class of drugs called calcium channel blockers. I've used for 30 years a
calcium channel blocker called nifedipine for many of my patients, not for the initial indication,
which is hypertension, but for a variety of other situations such as insect bites, allergic
reactions, even for singers to extend their upper range.

I use nifedipine when I do my seminars
because I have an IgA deficiency that affects the mucous membranes, that includes the larynx and
vocal cords and so on. And the verapamil prevents or inhibits inflammation of these structures. So
I'm not surprised, I use the nifedipine, but verapamil acts in a very similar manner.

So there is a good possibility that this may be of value. The important thing is that it probably is
virtually without adverse effects. I find that the nifedipine will not significantly lower blood
pressure in people who already have low blood pressure, although it will dramatically lower blood
pressure in people who are hypertensive.

That's basically the story, whether this will totally prevent further progression of type 1 diabetes
once the injury to beta cells begins, we don't know for sure. Whether it would pay to give this
product to high risk individuals, people, let's say siblings of a type 1 diabetic. So these trials
yet have to be performed, but as I said before, it's a benign drug and if it does the job, I
wouldn't expect much in the way of adverse effects.

Now on to our first question.

### Can my husband go on insulin for a short time to lose weight, and then go back to Metformin, Glimepiride, and Victoza after the weight loss safely? [short: Using insulin briefly to lose weight?]

### I have heavy callous growth on my feet. It is very serious and equally vexing. I do not know what to do about this problem. [short: What can I do about heavy calluses?]

And there's also a good chance that if you explore the uppers of the shoe, the top of the shoe, the

So if you have only one callous or two calluses, and they're on the upper or the side of the shoe,

adequately stretched.

yet another problem and might require intervention by a podiatrist to reduce the size of the

ended up with them losing a toe, foot, or leg. And in every case, it was a podiatrist trying to
remove a small callous. So that you have to be wary of.

The treatment of calluses is to get the pressure off the shoe, or reduce the pressure from the shoe.
