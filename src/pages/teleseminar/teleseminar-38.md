Hi, this is Dr. Bernstein with the October 2018 teleseminar.

Before we begin, I want to give you my usual caveat, namely that the answers I give are guesses. I
can't possibly get the full history, medical history of a patient who asked a question or do a
physical exam on a questioning patient, but I could give generalized answers that might be of
interest to the listenership in general and may not solve the particular individual problem.

First question

### Do you feel that some types of MODI (that's maturity onset diabetes of youth diabetics), can be treated with sulfonylureas? In previous teleseminars, you've shown your concern because of the risk of cardiotoxicity. [short: Can MODI be treated with sulfonylureas?]

Well, cardiotoxicity was not my major risk, it was one of the concerns. The major concern is that
sulfonylureas have been repeatedly shown to cause apoptosis or beta cell burnout.

They seem to work for maybe a year to four years and then they stop working. Well, they stop working
not because they're no longer able to work, but because the beta cells aren't there. They've
overworked beta cells by pushing them to produce loads of insulin compared to what they're capable
of doing because a diabetic beta cell has limited capability, so they're being overworked and they
burn out.

So it's a good way, taking sulfonylureas is a good way to convert a type two diabetic or a MODI
diabetic to a type 1 diabetic.

### Diabetic for 24 years, using 100 units of insulin daily (which is a large dose), overweight, 60 years of age, impossible to lose weight, following your food plan. Any suggestions? [short: How can I lose weight on 100 units of insulin?]

Well, first of all, I'd suggest that you read more of the book, my book, Diabetes Solution Than Just
the Food Plan. The food guidelines don't tell you how much to eat overall, which are based in part
on are you gaining weight, losing weight, building muscle, etc., and are you hungry.

Now it could be just eating too much, so that's the first thing to look at.

Another thing is if you're female and have PCOS, that can cause weight gain even though you're
hardly eating. At 60 years of age, I doubt that this is a problem, but it's a possibility.

You could be taking a medication or a supplement that raises blood sugar and therefore causes you to
take more insulin, and taking a lot of insulin can cause weight gain. I'm not telling you to cut
down the insulin if you need it for controlling your blood sugar though, but if you're taking a
medication that raises blood sugar, that's a potential problem.

You might also want to check for hypothyroidism, which can cause weight gain, so you should check
your free T3 by the methods that we've described on other videos.

Also there's the matter of exercise, if you're sedentary, you might find it easier to lose weight if
you exercise more, also easier to lower the insulin dose.

### Do you have an opinion on Victoza versus Ozempic, in particular whether their safety is equivalent? [short: Victoza versus Ozempic?]

I've not investigated the safety of these drugs. I do know that the whole category of GLP-1 agonists
can cause pancreatitis, and I've seen 3 cases caused by these drugs. The Ozempic is a once a
week GLP-1 agonist, and Victoza is one that you have to take several times a day.

If you read my book, Diabetes Solution, you'll know that I'm strictly using the GLP-1 agonist to
control appetite. Their direct effect on blood sugar is very small. One can almost call it trivial,
but the effect on overeating when it works is considerable. It could have a great effect on your
average blood sugar.

If you ask me which of the two drugs, the once a week or the twice a day one, is more effective for
overeating, I would say that in my experience it's the twice a day one. The once a week one had no
effect, and the reason probably relates to the reason many appetite suppressants wear off after a
while. You become tolerant of them, and if you're injecting an appetite suppressant that's working
all day long, day after day, you're going to become tolerant to it rapidly, whereas with Victoza,
you only take it for special occasions.

Many of my patients who overeat between dinner and bedtime will take a shot of Victoza before
dinner. It will start working after dinner, and it will curb their appetite until bedtime.

I don't recommend the Ozempic from the point of view of appetite control.

As far as safety goes, they're both equally safe. The pancreatitis is relatively rare, but it does
occur. And the way to find out if you're getting pancreatitis is to, at the first sign of any
gastrointestinal disturbance, check for your serum lipase level. You can also check your pancreatic
amylase level, and that will make a quick differential diagnosis between pancreatitis and other
causes.

### Thanks to your diet, I managed to reduce my A1c from 6.4% to 5.6% within 12 months. I also lost 25 pounds (11 kg), but my total cholesterol went up 60 and my LDL went up, total cholesterol went up to 260 and LDL went up to 175, my HDL is 60, my triglycerides are 122. What would you recommend in this case, cut out fat? [short: Cholesterol rise on a low-carb diet?]

First of all, and I've said this over and over, and I guess this may be a new listener or new
viewer, total cholesterol is not a predictor of heart disease, nor is total LDL. High triglycerides
are a good predictor, low HDL is a good predictor.

When someone goes on a low carbohydrate diet, what we find is that the LDL gets transformed from the
small dense or highly adhesive and potentially toxic LDL to the large buoyant LDL, which does not
stick to arteries, so if you follow a low carbohydrate diet, there's a good chance that your LDL
gets transformed to the good LDL.

We only measure LDL particle size, I do not measure total LDL because particle size tells you if
your LDL is a predictor of heart risk. If it's small and dense, then it's a good predictor. Also we
test for oxidized LDL because that's a good predictor, so if you want to look at oxidized LDL and
LDL particle size, then you're looking at something that has a reasonable information.

Certainly do not cut down on fat, I'm not advocating that you deliberately go out and eat fat, but
fat is not the cause of heart disease, it appears to be carbohydrate, and you get the details on
this if you've watched my other videos.

### Can long term use of metformin cause dementia?

Well usually it doesn't, but it can via one mechanism. Metformin can bind to the intestinal
receptors for vitamin B12 and prevent the absorption of vitamin B12.

Now thus far I've found only one patient where long term use of metformin caused a reduction in his
serum B12, I should say his whole body B12, and you can test for this.

A low body B12 can cause dementia, the brain needs B12, so what we do when we put someone on
metformin, I get a baseline level initially of serum B12 level but also homocysteine and
methylmalonic acid because these values go up when you have a low total body B12.

So I have one patient who just recently had normal serum B12, normal methylmalonic acid but his
homocysteine has gone up in the year or so that he started metformin.

So right off the bat we're going to prevent the malabsorption of B12 by giving him a calcium pill,
one Tums pill to chew with each metformin tablet and I might add that we don't give generic
metformin, we give the brand Glucophage.

So if you take these precautions you don't have to worry about the dementia.

### Dr. Bernstein, I've read that when following a low-carb diet, fats should make up 75-80% of all calories consumed. Does that seem reasonable to you? [short: Should fats be 75-80% of calories on low carb?]

No, it doesn't. I don't believe in deliberately pushing fats on people.

I think that we should do what our ancestors did. Our ancestors who lived off game or if they were
on the coast off fish should eat whatever fat comes with the meat or fish that they're eating.

To deliberately seek out fats or vegetable oils and things like that usually causes problems.

I've seen a number of new patients who come in on what they call paleo diets where it's low
carbohydrate usually but they're also cutting way back on protein and eating loads of fat and they
have protein malnutrition.

I certainly don't advocate deliberately going out and pushing fats.

### I'm a type 1, little beta cell function. I'm under 50 grams of carb most days. Most days I have 0.5 to 2 blood ketones with blood glucose between 80 and 100. Do I have to worry about hypo episodes because my brain can use the ketones for energy? [short: Do ketones protect against hypoglycemia?]

This is a good question and when we talk about the classic situation where humans are using ketones
for energy, it's when they're starving out in the wild or in more time when they're not getting
enough food and when you're starving, you make less insulin and that's why you start using your own
body for nutrition. You start breaking down your body fat and also your body proteins whereas
diabetics who inject insulin have just the opposite situation.

They'll get hypoglycemia from too much insulin. So you're talking about two separate situations.

The brain that's existing on no food, the ketones can keep the brain alive but there's no insulin or
virtually no insulin so they're not protecting you from hypoglycemia. Don't count on that.

### I am 37 years old. After 8 months of going vegan, I'm having digestive problems and have been diagnosed with chronic pancreatitis. Medical doctors are suggesting whole foods and low-fat diet. What's your recommendation? [short: What should I eat with chronic pancreatitis?]

If you have pancreatitis, you want to keep the part of the pancreas that makes enzymes quiet. One of
the enzymes that the pancreas makes is lipase which suggests fats.

In pancreatitis, the pancreas is leaking enzymes into its own structure rather than into the
intestines and it's self-digesting. So if you eat a lot of fats, it's going to make a lot of lipase
and more aggressively digest itself and make the pancreatitis more severe.

So absolutely you should have less fats with pancreatitis but you also should have less carbohydrate
in general and going vegan is really inviting more carbohydrate.

### Given the overall objective of targeting normal blood sugar levels in diabetes solution (that's my book). I'm curious if type 2s would do this because it's the primary objective or because it's a secondary proxy for the primary goal of minimizing insulin response. [short: Is normal blood sugar the goal, or minimizing insulin?]

The primary goal should be to normalize blood sugars so that you don't develop the long-term
consequences of type 2 diabetes. A secondary goal is indeed to preserve beta cell function so that
your diabetes will be easy to treat.

If you don't preserve beta cell function and lose it over time, you'll eventually become a type 1
diabetic and have to take multiple shots of insulin and that makes life a lot more complicated and
unless you go to extreme measures as I do for myself, you're not going to be able to readily control
your blood sugars.

So you want to both preserve both beta cells but certainly you want to prevent the long-term
complications caused by high blood sugars.

### I have type 2 diabetes. I'm well controlled. When I exercise, my blood sugars go up to 150 to 175 and it takes some time afterwards for it to come down. I have peripheral neuropathy in my feet. It feels more numb when my glucose readings are above 110. Is there anything I can do to keep my glucose numbers in check during and after exercise? [short: How can I stop exercise from raising blood sugar?]

Yes, there are several things you could do and the first thing is to read my book because this
question is answered in the book.

Number one, you don't exercise during the first four hours of the day when the dawn phenomenon is
active. There's a good chance that your blood sugars will go up because insulin is being cleared
from your bloodstream by the liver at that time of the day.

Another possibility is to take rapid-acting glucose three hours before the exercise. That's the
brand name for Metformin and there is a rapid-acting one and it might prevent the blood sugar
increase.

One thing that I use quite frequently is propranolol. I find that kids tend to have high blood
sugars after a game due to the emotional increase in stress hormones like epinephrine. This
particular writer probably has high blood sugars because of stress hormones nearly caused by the
exercise and if you can block the epinephrine, you might be able to block the blood sugar increase
so you can take 5 or 10 mg of propranolol, rapid-acting propranolol, one to two hours
before the exercise. That's probably the easiest solution.

Another possibility would be to take a small amount of Humalog by injection. By small, I have
patients who use diluted Humalog and might take what amounts to an eighth or a tenth of a unit of the
undiluted and they might take that about a half an hour before the exercise.
