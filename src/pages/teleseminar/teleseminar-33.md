### I'd like to get your input on wound healing for Type 1 diabetes. It seems my wounds heal slower and tend to leave scars. Is there anything you could recommend besides reducing my hemoglobin A1c? [short: How can I improve wound healing?]

Well, this is a difficult question for me to answer because I served in the wound care clinic of a
major medical center for 30 plus years, and I was director of the clinic for about 7 years. So
there's so much to know about helping wounds to heal. I'll try to take the most important things.

First of all, if you have an open wound, you should not apply hydrogen peroxide or any form of
iodine. Both these agents destroy the macrophages that facilitate healing. Those are white blood
cells that help with healing process.

The topical antiseptic that I prefer is called gentian violet. You can get it from
your pharmacy if they don't have it, stop it, cut it. It will do several things, calm wounds and
therefore act sort of as a Band-Aid to keep them from being exposed to further pathogens. If the
wounds are wet, it will tend to dry the wounds. It is staphylocidal. The usual pathogen that affects
the skin is staphylopoxide, and this stuff kills staphylopoxide, so it serves multiple purposes.

The one big hazard is that if you get it on your clothing, it cannot be removed. There's no cleaner
who can get gentian violet off your clothes. It's a deep purple color.

Another tip is to be very fast in caring for the wound. You don't wait to see what's going to
happen. You clean it off right away, and you apply whatever the appropriate treatment is, and I
can't tell you for every wound what the treatment is going to be.

Many people and many physicians prescribe bacitracin ointment, which is nothing more than a
hypoallergenic petroleum jelly. It's nice for keeping the wound moist, but it's unlikely to kill
infectious organisms. The antibiotic ointment that we prefer is Mupirocin, which goes by the brand
name Bactroban and several other brand names. It's a broad-spectrum antibiotic, but there's a
potential problem with that.

You don't want to use it for more than two weeks because it'll kill
most of the organisms on the wound, but those that survive after, say, two weeks, are going to be
resistant, and they may be resistant to other antibiotics. The usual hazard with broad-spectrum
antibiotics in general is to be very careful and not use them excessively, not use them too often.
So the gentian violet would be preferable in many situations to using even the Mupirocin.

Another tip is to keep, if the wound is not bleeding, you want to keep pressure off it because by
pressing on a wound, you compress the capillaries that bring in new blood to heal the wound. This is
a major problem that many of the wound care clinics have visited where there are foot wounds, wounds
on the bottom of the foot, and the clinics do all kinds of treatment for the wound and then have the
patient go out and walk, putting pressure on the wound so that it won't heal. So the wound has
technical pressure.

If it's on your foot, there are special shoes, special cast boots that can be modified to take the
pressure off the wound site, but even when modifying them, there are tricks. What I used to do was
put felt pads inside the cast boots, but you cannot, say, cut a circular hole in a felt pad because
you'll be pressing on the wound from the outside all around it, the acumen, the tourniquet,
preventing blood flow in. So I would make U-shaped cuts in the felt to take the pressure off the
wound so that blood could still come in and heal the wound, so there are many tricks.

It's a sensible but complicated business to properly treat wounds, and many of the centers I visit,
as I said, are not doing proper jobs.

### Can you compare the relative pluses and minuses of Tresiba insulin versus Levemir?

The major difference is that Tresiba lasts longer. For most type 2 diabetics, I find that they can
get away with taking it only once per day, whereas with Levemir, they may have to take it twice a
day. For type 1 diabetics, both Levemir and Tresiba usually must be taken twice a day.

Now, I have early type 1s who are still in the honeymoon period, where we've caught them early
enough that they can get away with Tresiba once a day, and usually a very small dose. But if you had
type 1 for a long time and have burned out your beta cells because your blood is not all wrong, then
you probably need to receive it twice a day.

In my case, not only do I take it twice a day, I take a
bedtime dose and my morning dose is about 3 or 4 hours before I arise in the morning. In
other words, I take my morning dose in the middle of the night because of the dawn phenomenon, and
most full type 1s have to take the Tresiba twice in the middle of the night. But that also applies
for the Levemir. The Tresiba does a better job.

One potential disadvantage to the Tresiba is it's probably harder to get. Not all pharmacies or
insurance plans will cover it. The manufacturer does have coupons that will pay the insurance
co-payment, but it will not cover it if the insurance pays nothing. So essentially, the one
disadvantage to Tresiba is it may be harder to get, it may even be more expensive.

### What do you consider industrial-sized doses of insulin?

Well, I would say probably about one and a half to two times what a non-diabetic your size, your

There was one – I can only think of one study that was published on this subject and that was
