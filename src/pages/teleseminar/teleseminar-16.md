### You have mentioned that the use of Tresiba insulin should be twice daily. Why twice a day as opposed to once a day? [short: Tresiba insulin why twice a day?]

Well, first
of all, Tresiba insulin is probably the longest acting insulin that we have available at this
moment in time, which is the end of 2016. It will, for a person who makes no insulin
at all, last perhaps three quarters of a day before it starts tapering off. So, for type
1 diabetics or anyone who's lost almost all of his beta cells, twice a day injections
are necessary.

It just doesn't last long enough to cover the whole day. There are some
individuals like type 2 diabetics who are still making a fair amount of insulin but
need a little bit of help who can get Tresiba once a day.

For example, I have type 2 diabetics
who need some help over the part of the day that they're having meals. And we give them
a little bit of Tresiba in the morning to cover the eating part of the day and they
get none at night and they do fine,
because their beta cells are getting enough of a rest
during the day that overnight they can make enough insulin to prevent gluconeogenesis
that would raise their blood sugar overnight. So that's the reason. There are some people,
as I said, type 2 diabetics who may only need it once a day and that's usually in the morning.

### What are your feelings about the Omnipod insulin pump being used by someone who has severe gastroparesis, as it would allow extending a bolus over several hours? [short: Insulin pump for severe gastroparesis?]

Well, that's a clever idea,
and it would work if gastroparesis were predictable. But the big problem about gastroparesis or
the hallmark of gastroparesis is its unpredictability. That's why we cannot use gastric emptying
studies as a test for gastroparesis because sometimes the stomach empties normally and
sometimes it doesn't when you have gastroparesis.

It's only in the very extreme cases that a
gastric emptying study will disclose gastroparesis every time you do it. So trying to preset
an insulin bolus spread out over several hours, the setting is going to be different
every time you eat, so you can't predict it in advance.

And the real game plan probably
is to use medications to speed the stomach emptying, also to modify the meal so that
the food goes down easily. People with severe gastroparesis usually need liquid meals. So
by making the meal emptying more predictable, you can be more likely to have success. Now,
if you want to do everything you can to make the emptying normalized or improved, and then
use an insulin pump to adjust things, it's a possibility.

The one big problem is that
once you have scar tissue at your pump sites, the absorption of the insulin from the pump

you might be better off injecting a mixture of regular insulin and NPH, which slows down

blood sugars are high, collagen gets glycated. Glucose sticks to it, makes it stiff, makes
it get thicker. And it presses on the median nerve in the wrist, causing the tingling or
pain or even paralysis. And this is different than neuropathy.

Normalizing blood sugars
does not get rid of the glycated collagen because collagen has a very long half-life
in the body. In a young, healthy adult, non-diabetic, collagen has a half-life of 15 years. In an
older person, it might have a half-life of 30 years. So it's going to be around a long
time.

### Have you ever seen an elevated sed rate in diabetes?

measured by putting some whole blood in a long, narrow tube and measuring how rapidly
the column of red cells drops in the serum of the blood over the period of an hour. And
the higher the rate of fall of red blood cells, the higher the sedimentation rate.

And there
are a number of autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, including infections, that cause
elevated sed rate. And diabetes per se, to my knowledge, does not. But I have encountered
many patients with gum and tooth infections, with lupus and other autoimmune diseases who
