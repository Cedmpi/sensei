### Special subject: Protein and kidney disease

Before we start answering questions today, I'm going to cover a special subject, namely the fact
that many of the people who send in questions are told by their doctors that they have to reduce the
protein in their diet because protein is bad for the kidneys. This happens over and over, and I'll
tell you a little bit about where this came from and my involvement in this.

The hazard of proteins was initially set forth by an investigator in the 1970s and 80s named Barry
Brenner of Yale. He appeared at a meeting of the New York Diabetes Association, which was a chapter
of the American Diabetes Association, around 1985, maybe 1984 even.

I was freshly out of training, and I had just
applied for membership in the professional section of the New York chapter of the ADA. So my
application required that you be practicing for at least a year. So it must have been 1984 I had
been practicing a year and submitted my application, and Barry Brenner comes to announce his great
discovery with diabetic rats. He was on a tour, probably sponsored by Yale, to publicize this very
important information, and it even got into the newspapers.

If you look at the publicity campaign and its medical, you can get it into the newspapers. He had
kept rats at a blood sugar of 250 mg/dL, and he had two groups of rats, those on a standard rat chow diet
and those on a high protein diet. When he announced the diet, the blood sugar clamping, at 250, he
said that he got the target of 250 from the local physicians in the ADA, and he got a consensus, 
and it looked like the ADA physicians liked blood sugars of 250 for their diabetics.

And he asked the audience, would you guys all agree with this? And raise your hands if you agree.
And everyone but me raised their hands. There are a lot of people there.

And then he said, any people disagree, and I get up and I say that diabetics are entitled to the same blood sugars as
non-diabetics. And I get booed for about a whole minute, and that was the end of my application for
professional membership, because the meeting was held immediately after Brenner's presentation, and
all the voters on membership were upset by my comment.

Anyway, what Barry Brenner found is that
those on the high protein diet died of diabetic kidney disease twice as fast as the rats on the rat
chow diet.

Well, I was around that time working on a study with a team at New York Hospital led by John Laroff,
who was the guy who gave publicity to the role of the kidney in hypertension. And he's the fellow
who developed the first ACE inhibitors, which is a class of drugs for treating hypertension and also
kidney disease. And Laroff had a research fellow with him from Scotland, his name was Gordon Bell.

And Gordon Bell was doing a study on atrial natriuretic factor, and he wanted to compare the poorly
controlled diabetics at New York Hospital with my diabetics who were well-controlled. My patients
were the only well-controlled diabetics he could find in the country.

So he was studying that.

And 5 years after Brenner made his presentation, Bell makes a presentation to the National Kidney
Foundation at their annual meeting in Washington, D.C. And lo and behold, he calls me from
Washington that Brenner made a presentation and that Brenner's presentation was made this time with
rats at normal blood sugars. I guess he kept them at around 100. And there, all of the rats lived
normal rat lives, none of them developed rat kidney disease. The study did not get published in any
journal except the archives of that meeting where it was just an [abstract of the study](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/113120).

And there was no publicity campaign to spread the word that
the culprit is high blood sugar, not high protein.

So that's the sort of thing that happens in research studies. And it turns out that this blaming
kidney disease on protein is one of the myths like the cholesterol myth.

### What do you tell your patients and how quickly to lower their A1c with relation to retinopathy worsening? [short: Retinopathy worsening when quickly lowering A1c?]

Well, what I tell them relates to a blood test for IGF-1. If IGF-1 is elevated and you already have
retinopathy, it's been shown that you can make the retinopathy worse if your blood sugar is lowered
rapidly. Now, how rapid is rapidly? From what I recall of the study, it did not indicate how fast
you go from what blood sugar to what lower blood sugar.

So it's a warning to be cautious if your
IGF-1 is already elevated. So certainly, if you have retinopathy, your doctor should test you for
IGF-1. And then if it's elevated, you lower the blood sugar slowly, not suddenly.
