Welcome to another session of Dr. Bernstein's Diabetes University.
This time we're going to talk about one of the many myths that are out there about diabetes
and this particular myth is called the Somoji Phenomenon.

About nine months ago, I was interviewing a new patient and she told me that her endocrinologist
had told her that whenever blood sugars drop low, they rebound very high and that they
can go up by 500 or more due to a magical effect invented by or discovered by a Chinese
doctor called the Somoji Phenomenon.

That came to my mind when I received in the mail this weekend a multicolor brochure advertising
a teleseminar on hypoglycemia for physicians and one of the highlighted subjects was the
Somoji Phenomenon.
The speakers in this teleseminar were for the most part former officers and editors
of the ADA and I get this in the mail and I'm astounded that this myth has permeated
so deeply.

Now, when I was a kid, we were told that there was a Hungarian biochemist named Mikael Somoji
who came to the USA in the 20s, ended up at Hebrew Children's Hospital in St. Louis and
had invented a more rapid test for blood sugar.
More rapid probably was an hour instead of three hours back in those days.

In any event, it was his belief that when children woke up in the morning with high
blood sugars that they must have had hypoglycemia during the night.
He had some clues that suggested this might have happened but no hard evidence.
Remember that they did not have blood sugar meters and to do a blood sugar would mean
being present in the middle of the night, waking a child, drawing some blood from a
vein which was difficult, and then processing it for at least an hour if not more.

So this was really a speculation and he had no hard evidence of this.
But I was taught this when I was a child and I believed it until I got my big three pound (1.4 kg)
blood sugar meter in 1969 and started measuring blood sugars.

And I would frequently have very low blood sugars, in fact for reasons that I'll explain
to you in another session, sometimes it went down to zero.
But after I had a low blood sugar, I would eat everything in the cabinet to try to get
it back up fast.
So I would eat a quart of ice cream or a jar of peanut butter or both or a jar of jelly
or you name it.
It could be a box of Oreos and my blood sugar would slowly go up but two, three, four hours
later I would be up to a thousand.

Now the modern meters, you can't measure blood sugars of a thousand but in those days
we could because although it only went I think up to 500, it was a timed test.
You would leave the blood on the strip and then wash it off after 60 seconds.
What I would do then is I'd leave it on for 30 seconds and wash it off and if I had a
reading of 500 I would double it or if I had a reading of or I might in some cases
do it for only 20 seconds and multiply it by three.
In any event, I repeatedly had blood sugars over a thousand and it seemed obvious that
it had to do with what I had eaten.

So I rapidly found out how I could get in those days glucose tablets and the first tablets
I was able to get actually came from Canada and the United Kingdom.
They were called dextrozole tablets and each tablet would raise my blood sugar I believe
15 milligrams per deciliter and of course I over time switched over to liquid glucose
which I advocate for anybody who's in a hurry.
One teaspoon of this will raise my blood sugar by seven and I will use a shot glass calibrated
teaspoons for precise adjustment of my blood sugar so I don't get any more rebounds.

So the Somoji phenomenon clearly wasn't true.
It was phony.
Now a very smart physician from Southwestern Texas University did a literature search looking
at every article he could about the Somoji phenomenon and there was no evidence.
None of these presented cases where they measured blood sugars and measured what the patient
consumed and the patient consumed a precise amount of glucose etc.
They only went up if they had huge amounts of carbohydrate.

So he wrote an article it was published in archives of internal medicine in 1984 and
he gave it an appropriate name namely the Somoji phenomenon sacred cow or bull.
So that was a great title and with that we'll close this session. Thank you.
