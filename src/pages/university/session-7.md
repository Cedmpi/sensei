This is Dr. Bernstein's Diabetes University, session number seven.

We're going to talk about cardiovascular exercises.  
As you know, poorly controlled diabetes predisposes to congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction,
and many other vascular and cardiovascular diseases, which could be prevented if you
had normal blood sugars and were following a low carbohydrate diet.

But many of the viewers of this session have not been doing that.
And while they're switching over, we could help them to maybe improve their cardiac
fitness.

Now the kind of exercise that I've been using for about 40 or 50 years is very strenuous
and very taxing, and therefore potentially dangerous if you already have narrowing of
your arteries or more significantly, if you have clumps of plaque in your coronary arteries
and you get your heart rate up, you can knock them loose, they could go upstream and cause
a blockage causing a heart attack.

So this kind of exercise is potentially dangerous.
Therefore, I ask all of my diabetic patients who are over the age of 40 to get two imaging
studies to see if they already have significant heart disease.
One imaging study is the coronary artery calcium score, which measures the total mass
of plaque deposits in the coronary arteries.

And the other is CT angiography, which takes images of little clumps of plaque that are
blocking or partially blocking coronary arteries.
These clumps are especially dangerous because they can be knocked loose if you have a very
rapid heart rate.

And the game plan here is a rapid heart rate.
Now for those who already have elevated calcium scores or blockages, I recommend going into
this very slowly in consultation with your cardiologist.
You might start at a heart rate that's maybe 10% above your resting heart rate.
On the other hand, if you have no evidence of coronary artery disease and if you're diabetic
and have had normal blood sugars since the onset of the diabetes, you know, you read
my book early on, you can probably move more rapidly.
I did not invent this system.

It was actually invented by Nathan Pritikin many years ago.
He had, I recall, something called rest angina where he would get chest pain just from sitting
around without doing anything.

And he got himself to get up and walk to the chair a few feet away and sit down.
And he would do that every hour.
And eventually he'd increase the distance between chairs.
And over a period of years, he became a marathon runner.
I believe he ended up dying of leukemia and out of heart disease.
In any event, the ball game is to gradually get the heart rate up.

Now I use a recumbent bicycle.  
You can use a regular bicycle.  
You can use a treadmill.  
The trouble with treadmills is that as you get your heart rate up, you're going to go
faster and faster and you're going to be banging your knees more and more.
And you're going to increase the hazard of injuring your knees.
In my experience, most long-term runners have eventually injured their knees.
So this is a hazard you want to avoid.

With the recumbent bicycle, the legs are at the level of the heart, which is a particularly
safe way of doing the job.

Now you can calculate your theoretical maximum heart rate, which is 211 minus 64 percent
of your age.

And you could set that as at least a theoretical target.
If you already have heart disease, you're going to go much less than this.
You might start only 10 percent above your resting heart rate.

If you're in good health, no evidence of heart disease, you might shoot for, let's say, 60
or 70 percent of your theoretical maximum.
And then depending upon how much guts you have, either increase the heart rate by one
beat per minute per month or by two beats per minute per month.
And over a course of a year or more, depending upon how rapidly you're increasing your heart
rate, you'll get up to your theoretical maximum.

And as I did, you could eventually perhaps get beyond your theoretical maximum.
But for many people, there's an obstacle, particularly if you're either in very good
shape or as you get older.
I find that I have a difficulty getting to my theoretical maximum that I did not have
20 years ago.

I, let's say, I'm shooting for 160 beats per minute.
And I start off, my resting heart rate is about 68 beats per minute.
So I start off pedaling away under moderate resistance.
And my heart rate is going up.
I'll tell you a good way to measure your heart rate in a minute, but that comes later.
I see my heart rate going up and I get to maybe 115 beats per minute and I can't get
beyond it.

I'm hitting a wall.
So I stop and I rest for two minutes.
By the way, if your heart is healthy, you should be able to drop 42 beats per minute
after a rest of two minutes.

Well, did my heart rate even go up 42 beats?
Yeah, just about.
Anyway, I rest two minutes and now I try again and maybe I can get to 125 beats per minute.
And again, I hit a wall.
And I do this repeatedly.

It might take me a half an hour of starts and stops to get up to my target of 160.
Eventually, I'll get there, but it's been a lot of work.
The question then arises, how long do you stay there?
Well, I've done so much work getting up there that I don't want to stay there very long.
One, because I don't have that much time and two, because it's not fun.

Exercising at your theoretical maximum is not painful, but extremely uncomfortable.
So I might stay there a couple of minutes.
I've known some people who've stayed there 45 minutes.
I don't know how they can do it, but they're to be congratulated.
So that's basically the system.

The most important thing is starting out slowly if you're at risk and having this supervised
by a cardiologist or a physician who knows sports medicine.
I have a really neat way of tracking my heart rate.
I use a pulse meter made by a company called Polar, P-O-L-A-R.
It's a rubber strap that goes around my chest and has a snap-on sensor.
And it comes with a free app that I download to my iPad from the Apple App Store and you
can probably get apps for systems other than Apple.

And it's called Polar Beat.
That's the name of the app.
And it will display my heart rate in large numbers on my iPad.
And I can actually mount my iPad on my recumbent bike.
It has a little slot where you fit in the iPad.
And there in big letters before me is my changing heart rate.
So that's basically the system for a cardiovascular workout that's extremely effective.
And I've had many occasions to test it out.

For example, about 40 years ago before I became a physician, I used to go to medical meetings
to try to meet with doctors and convince them that diabetics should have normal blood sugars.
And I'd go out running with them.
Those days they used to go out in their silk shorts at six o'clock in the morning.
And even though I wasn't a runner, I had much more stamina than these other fellows, even
though they were younger than I was.

And I'd outpace them.
I'd go five miles more and then I'd come back.
So this kind of workout does give you considerable stamina.
And I recommend it highly.
