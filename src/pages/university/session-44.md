Our subject for today
is psoriasis. Why psoriasis? Well, when I started in practice, I used to draw my own
bloods, not on me, but on my patients. And when you draw blood on somebody, somehow you
end up holding their elbow while you stick the needle in. And I found that every patient
had a rough elbow. And rough elbows, I knew from personal experience, are characteristic
of psoriasis, which is a skin disorder where skin cells proliferate in a semi-uncontrolled
fashion. And the most common symptom is rough elbows.

So, when I felt a rough elbow, I'd
also look at their fingernails because it was very common to see changes in the nails
of people with psoriasis. And indeed, almost all of these people would have longitudinal
fine lines on their nails. I also noticed
something that I didn't know but learned, that the bulk of these people with psoriasis
had missing lunulae.

If you look at a normal finger, you'll see what looks like a quarter
of a moon at the base of the nail near the cuticle. And people with psoriasis have missing
moons or missing quarter moons. I have none on my fingers. I've had psoriasis since I
was 12 years old.

Another characteristic of psoriasis is shaggy cuticles. Another characteristic
is nails that break easily. Another characteristic is itchy scalp, very common. Another one would
be itchy nose, itchy ears.

Yet another one is perianal itching. Many years ago, a patient
asked me at a visit in a quiet voice if I could do anything about his perianal itching.
It was embarrassing to walk around scratching most of the day. So I said, let me feel your
elbows. And sure enough, he had psoriatic elbows. So I suggested that he use hydrocortisone
ointment that in this particular case worked. Now, I've had patients with psoriatic ears
where it looked like they had cauliflowers growing out of their ears. It was only one
patient. One patient had what was cauliflowers growing out of her ears that was skin that
had proliferated.

I had a man who had it all over his body and couldn't concentrate
on the training when he first came because he was itching all over and was busy scratching.
And we sent him to a dermatologist who had a light box in his office where you stand
inside a box that has ultraviolet B lighting, and that's a treatment for psoriasis. There
are many treatments for psoriasis, and not all of them work on everybody. The rough elbows
frequently respond merely to lubrication, and then it's a matter of which lubricant
will work. Well, a good lubricant would be olive oil or jojoba oil, but they might not
work for some people and might work for others. 1% hydrocortisone is an over-the-counter mild
steroid that you can use for about two weeks. After two weeks or so, it stops working, so it's
not something to be used every day chronically for the rest of your life. You would switch to
something else if the hydrocortisone works after maybe two weeks or so, use something else for a
month, and then they go back to the hydrocortisone.

There are so many things used for treating
psoriasis that I'm not going to even attempt to mention them, but I will mention that in the USA
there's a National Psoriasis Foundation. You can look them up on the internet, National Psoriasis
Foundation. They publish a monthly newsletter. They also publish a medical journal. By the way,
when I tried to point out that virtually a hundred percent of diabetics have psoriasis,
the medical journal, their journal wouldn't publish it. When I submitted it to the diabetes,
and they said to me, we're interested in psoriasis, not diabetes. When I submitted the same article to
major diabetes journals, they said we're interested in diabetes, not psoriasis. So,
I couldn't get it published. This is my first public disclosure of this, but I think I've
had two patients in 30 years who did not have psoriasis.

The newsletter of the National Psoriasis
Foundation lists many treatments. They even have a column called It Works for Me, where some people
tell what helped them. And what helps one person may or may not work on another person. So, there
are many, many treatments. Some of the extreme ones involve the use of monoclonal antibodies
that destroy a portion of the immune system that's causing the psoriasis, but can also cause an
immune deficiency.

There are things like salt water, all kinds of oils, steroids, and I mentioned
itchy nose. I have psoriasis, and tonight I have an itchy nose. Tomorrow I might have an itchy
scalp. The next day I might have itchy ears, and so on. So, if you go to the National Psoriasis
Foundation publication, I think they charge $20 or $40 a year for a membership, and the publication
is then free. And I'm sure if you're overseas, you can still join and go to their website. You'll
learn about the many treatments that are available. There are also dermatologists who
specialize in psoriasis. Not every dermatologist is an expert on psoriasis, and I found many who
make mistakes and don't know how to recognize it. So, that's about all I can tell you, but it
affects almost every diabetic.
