### My daughter has type 1, and we have been doing a low-carb diet for almost two years. Her LDL was high, 150. I heard that only 1 to 2 percent of diabetics will have an increase in LDL while on a low-carb diet. Do you know anything about this, and do you have a solution? [short: High LDL while on a low-carb diet?]

Well, usually, first of all, I've said this many times on our teleseminars and on the
diabetes university videos, LDL by itself is not really a marker of cardiac risk as
they used to think.

A better marker might be oxidized LDL, which you can measure, glycated LDL, which is only
measured in research laboratories, but more significantly even, small, dense LDL.

There are at least two flavors of LDL, actually a spectrum, but it's arbitrarily broken down
into two flavors, small dense and large buoyant.
And the large buoyant LDL is harmless, whereas the small dense LDL has indeed been shown
to be predictive of atherosclerosis.

Now, what usually happens when you normalize blood sugars is that the LDL, after a few
months, changes from small dense or cardiotoxic to large buoyant, which is benign.

And what probably happened in the case of your daughter is that not only did she have
this conversion, but she ended up with more of the large buoyant particles, which are
less dense, probably the total mass of LDL, because these are less dense, went down, even
though the total number of particles may have gone up.

In any event, the only way you could find anything more significant would be if at the
beginning you had checked for the particle density, and then on the follow-up blood draw,
I'd also looked at particle density.

I do this routinely on every new patient, so before a patient even comes in here, I
see his lab report, and I know whether he's mostly small dense or mostly large buoyant,
and we actually get a number for each of those.
So looking at total LDL just has no meaning.

Now, many doctors are using any increase in LDL as an excuse for not controlling blood
sugars or for not following low-carb diets, and it's just not a valid excuse, and it demonstrates
lack of knowledge of the true risk factors for heart disease.

### Can't find any docs to do an R-R interval test to diagnose gastroparesis. Is there any other test, like a gastric emptying test? [short: Any other test to diagnose gastroparesis?]

No, there isn't.  
In fact, a gastric emptying test is very problematical.

I, when I first started in practice, was doing gastric emptying studies whenever I suspected
gastroparesis, and typically I had to get four studies before I would get a positive
result, before I could make a diagnosis.  
We'd get 3 negatives, and then maybe we'd get a positive.
Now how did I get patients to afford 4 of these expensive studies, one after another?

It was because the country's authority on gastric emptying, Richard W. McCallum, was then
at Yale, which is not far from where I am, and he had a grant to do gastric emptying
studies, technetium scans, and it wouldn't cost my patients anything, and if I sent him
a patient and he got a negative, he might have them come back a few days later, do it
again, and we found that it might take four different studies before the delayed emptying
showed up, because the hallmark of gastroparesis is unpredictability.

If you get unpredictable low blood sugars after dinner and highs in the morning, where
it doesn't happen every night, but happens some nights, you can be pretty sure it's gastroparesis.

Now the next question is, who does RR studies?  
In response to this question, I did a search on the internet, and the first name that came
up was me, which amazed me, and there were so many responses, most of which were journal
articles and not the names of practitioners, and I didn't have time to look for all of
