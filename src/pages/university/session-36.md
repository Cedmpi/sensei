Hi, this is Dr. Bernstein, welcoming you to another session of Dr. Bernstein's Diabetes
University. Today, we're going to look at cardiac imaging studies and risk for heart
attacks.

For several months, our producer Dave Dykman has been after me to talk about
coronary artery calcium scores and similar imaging studies. I resisted him because this
scenario is constantly changing. There are always new things happening. But then the other day,
I received a notice from the insurance company that pays bills for one of my patients, pays
pharmacy bills, telling me that he was at high risk, I'm sorry, that he had a seven and a half
percent risk of dying from a heart attack in the next ten years, according to Framingham
guidelines.

Well, it happens, first of all, that this gentleman had recently had two studies that
I recommended. One was a coronary artery calcium score, which images all of the calcium that
appears in the lining of the arteries and actually in the walls of the arteries of the heart and has
a good correlation with heart attacks and other damage to the heart, especially caused by elevated
blood sugars.

We also did a CT angiogram on this patient, which shows any blockages on the inside
of the arteries. Calcium score shows calcium in the walls of the arteries. This shows obstruction
in the lumen of the artery, the part of the artery where the blood flows. And this man had clean
arteries both on the inside and in the muscles. So he had essentially no risk of heart attack.

Why did I do these studies on him? Because I thought that it would be a good idea for him to
engage in vigorous cardiac exercise, exercise that gets his heart rate up very high. I do this
myself. I'm 81 years old. I try to get my heart rate well above the theoretical maximum. And I
think it's a good idea for people who have healthy hearts to do this also. However, I don't want to
give them a heart attack by creating too demanding an exercise if they already have cardiac damage.

So that's why I do these tests before prescribing such exercise. Well, lo and behold, this guy was
free and clear, yet his insurer was insisting that he has to take statins to prevent a heart
attack. And they referred to Framingham guidelines. And the same day that I get the notice from the
insurance company, I read a headline that appeared in several publications that the Framingham
guidelines are excessive to the overstate CV risk, cardiovascular risk, by 78%, which means that 78%
of the people they say are at risk are not at risk, which means the bulk of the people they say are at
risk are not at risk.

This is the sort of thing that's been going on with regard to recommendations
about heart disease, diet, etc., over the past 20 years or so. So how do you interpret all
of this? Well, if you're wondering, are you at risk, looking at the standard tests that the
Framingham study was looking at, is looking at something that's invalid, looking at total
cholesterol, LDL, other than oxidized LDL, and so on, are not looking at reasonable risk factors,
as I've pointed out before.

Heart disease is more accurately predicted by inflammatory markers,
things like Lipoprotein(a), fibrinogen, etc. But if you want to be more precise in your
estimate, you do an imaging study. However, no sooner did I say this a couple of years ago,
and one of my patients had it done, I suggested it because we wanted him to exercise, when he
started doing it every three years, which is ridiculous, because the radiation for these
studies is not insignificant, it's quite significant. You don't want to be repeating them over and over
again. You do it once, and then you do everything right to prevent an increase in your risk, and
that might involve the exercise if it's safe for you, it might involve a low carbohydrate diet,
it might involve normal blood sugars if you're diabetic, etc., etc.

But I don't recommend you
get irradiated every couple of years. If you want to do it every 10 years, maybe that might be
reasonable. Anyway, that's the story about calcium scores and coronary CT angiography.

Now, contrary to what many cardiologists have told me, I've seen a lot of side effects from statins
in patients who came in for the first time using statins. They get muscle cramps, some have
tiredness, weakness, lack of energy. We've seen a lot of unpleasant effects, so I don't think that
statins should be used if there's no justification. And in this case, where the insurer is recommending
statins and there's no justification, it's typical of what's been happening because of the Framingham
studies.
