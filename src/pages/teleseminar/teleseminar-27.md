This is Dr. Bernstein with our November 2017 teleseminar.

Before we begin, I want to remind you that the answers I give are geared for the general
audience and may not really solve the problem of the person who was asking the questions.

Reason for this is I haven't examined that person, I haven't taken a full history, and
I'm just guessing at an answer, but it does give you a chance to see the logic involved
in coming to a tentative conclusion.

First question,

### I'm a female, 58 years old, 120 pounds (54 kg), I was a gestational diabetic. If I eat a half cup of ice cream, my blood sugar is over 200. My fasting blood sugar is usually between 112 and 119. I reported this to my doc, but she thinks my levels are okay. How can I convince her that I really have abnormal blood sugars? [short: Convincing my doctor that my blood sugars are abnormal?]

Well, probably the answer is to get an oral glucose tolerance test, which she can administer.
You drink 100 grams of glucose in a solution, and it's a purchase solution that comes in
a bottle.

Many doctors can administer this test, and then they do finger stick blood sugars every
half hour, and if after two hours you're over 200, you're by definition diabetic.
If you're over 146, I believe you're called prediabetic or something like that.

That's probably a good way to convince her.

### I started Victoza less than a month ago, and my blood sugars went down immediately. I increased the dosage on Monday, and they have gone down even more. Can you please comment on the pros and cons of Victoza, whether it is easy to come off it, possible adverse effects, permanent damage, et cetera? [short: Pros and cons of Victoza?]

I don't think you could call it an addicting drug, and the main long-term effect is the
possibility of pancreatitis, which is a very low possibility.

Now, the American Diabetes Association and other organizations that accept paid advertising
from the manufacturer say that pancreatitis from this drug is impossible, but I've seen
it on three people so far.

What you could do to play it safe is ask your doctor to check your serum levels of pancreatic
lipase and pancreatic amylase periodically, maybe after the first week and then after
the first month, and if they become elevated or even if they increase, you have a warning,
and hopefully you won't have symptoms, which are usually abdominal distress, but again,
this is quite rare.

I use Victoza for my patients who cannot control their overeating.
I use it to control appetite, and it can be quite effective for about half the people
you try it on and therefore a very valuable tool in controlling blood sugars.

The direct effect of Victoza on blood sugar is marginal, it's quite small, but the effect
via appetite can be considerable.

The one catch, however, is that many of the people who use it, not all, but let's say
about a third, develop a tolerance after a while and their appetite returns, but even
then you can switch to a similar agent like Byetta and not have a tolerance to the Byetta
and then wait X months or a year, and if you become tolerant to the Byetta, you might be
able to go back to Victoza or maybe even to Amylin.

All this is covered in my book, Diabetes Solution.

And then, she should eventually be comfortable with blood sugars of 75 or 80 for a 13 year
old girl.

That's basically the best advice I can give you right now.
Hopefully she'll get used to blood sugars of 75 or 80.

There are so many causes of cramps.

cramps go away.

### How does the ketogenic diet compare with yours for type 2 diabetics and do you recommend it?

Well, everyone's definition of the ketogenic diet seems to be different.

Dr. Atkins originally recommended what he called the ketogenic diet for obese people
to be able to track whether they're metabolizing fat because when you metabolize fat, you produce
ketones and you can measure ketones in your urine.
As long as you have ketones in your urine most of the day, you're burning fat.

Do I see a purpose in measuring ketones or producing ketones in the treatment of diabetes?

Not really, I haven't measured my urine for ketones since I started measuring my blood
sugar in 1969 because it's impossible for me to get ketoacidosis doing what I say you
should do in my book, so why should I be measuring ketones?

In fact, most humans when fasting will produce ketones in their urine and many humans just
by fasting overnight will awaken with ketones in the urine the next morning, so I just see
no point in testing now.

Then what is a ketogenic diet?

I've seen different versions, different books, different internet sites and the one thing
I've seen many of these advocates, especially people who are selling books via the internet,
they seem to advocate a lot of fat, frequently fruit which is certainly not good for diabetics
and frequently too little protein.

So I've seen people come in in pretty poor physical
condition on so-called ketogenic diets that they had read about that were telling them
to cut back on protein, so this is a whole strange ball game with the same word being
applied to a lot of diets.
