### Special subject: SGLT2 inhibitor side effects

The first one relates to SGLT2 antagonists. At my last seminar, I mentioned that in spite of studies
showing that these agents to lower blood sugar and type 2 diabetics were supposedly shown to reduce
the incidence of heart disease,

I expected other complications to occur because I thought the idea of putting a lot of glucose
through the kidneys of diabetics into the urine was just unnatural and likely to cause problems. Lo
and behold, we have an article in one of the endocrinology journals, which has been some subject in
general news to the effect that gangrene cases surge in patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors. This
is gangrene of the feet that frequently leads to amputations. 

I certainly would not want to treat one of my patients with a medication that increases
the incidence of amputations. As I suspected previously, I would expect more news about serious side
effects from the use of these agents. This is just my suspicion, my expectation, not necessarily going to turn
out to be true, but it's been verified with this gangrene study.

### Special subject: Evaluating blood glucose meters

One other special subject that relates to several recent studies of blood sugar meters and blood
sugar test systems like continuous glucose monitors, frequently the results of these studies will
highlight one particular meter system as being superior, but you have to be careful. You have to
look and see who paid for the study or who was responsible for the study.

If it was a meter maker, you could be sure to expect that that company's meter is going to come out
on top. Another thing to look at is did the study disclose at what blood sugars the various meters
were superior or inferior, and most of the studies do not disclose that. You could have a meter that
was very accurate at blood sugars of 200, and that would make the ADA very happy because they like
to see an A1C of 7 for most diabetics, which is an average blood sugar of around 200. But for my
patients, we have an average blood sugar in the 80s or for kids in the 70s. We want a meter that's
most accurate at those values.

If the study doesn't show you where the meter is accurate, it's not telling you very much. At least
for the time being, go by the recommendations that I make because we usually do our own studies of
any new meters that come out.

Now we'll get down to the questions.

### Are eye floaters or cataracts caused or exacerbated by type 2 diabetes?

Well, they can be, but they also can be unrelated. For example, if we look at eye floaters, you can,
if you are a high myope, that is you're very nearsighted, the shape of your eyeball will be more
egg-shaped instead of spherical. As you age, that will put tension on the vitreous humor in the back
of the eye that contacts the retina.

Because of the shape of the eye, you might have vitreous tears where the vitreous is tearing the
retina at the edges and that can lead to floaters appearing and that may have nothing to do with
blood sugar. On the other hand, you can get floaters for a variety of other reasons including micro
hemorrhages and whatnot that are caused by blood sugars.

As far as cataracts are concerned, many people get cataracts just through aging and our people are
not diabetic, but the lens of the eye which is where the cataracts form is an aldose, the cells are
aldose reductase cells, the cells of the retina and these are cells that can admit glucose in the
absence of insulin unlike let's say muscle or fat cells and you can have very high levels of glucose
in these cells and they can be rapidly damaged by high blood sugars causing cataracts.

### Have you seen any research on using stem cells to treat type 1 diabetes?

Well there's been lots of research.

There are two approaches. One approach is to inject people with either their own stem cells or stem
cells from animals or stem cells from another human and these attempts have been very unsuccessful.

The wiser approach is to somehow or other get the stem cells that everyone naturally has in their
body, the stem cells for beta cells, the precursors of beta cells that make insulin and we know what
some of these stem cells are.

We know for example that the alpha cells that make glucagon can transform to beta cells so if we can
find a way of inducing alpha cells to transform into beta cells and remember diabetics still have
alpha cells even though they may have destroyed virtually all of their beta cells and indeed some
studies have shown certain hormones or chemicals that can be administered to diabetic animals that
get their alpha cells to turn into beta cells so that approach is being studied.

Then it's been discovered that the endothelial cells of the ducts that conduct digestive enzymes
from the pancreas that the cells that line these ducts can become beta cells if exposed to the right
substances so studies are being done on administering the substances to get the endothelial cells to
become beta cells.

One ongoing problem is that if you convert these cells to beta cells once they start making insulin,
the immune system is still capable of making antibodies that destroy the beta cells but there may be
solutions to that problem.

For example, if you find something that rapidly transforms the endothelial cells in large amounts to
beta cells, you might just keep doing it even as the antibodies are produced or you might have a way
of down the road reducing the production of antibodies so research is being done but we don't have a
solution yet and I suspect that more money is being put into the transplantation of stem cells which
I suspect is not going to be the answer. I think the answer is going to end up in stimulating your
own stem cells to become beta cells.

### I have had type 1 since 3 years of age. I'm now 45 years old (so this person is a survivor). I've had a high pulse rate of 100 to 120 for a long time since my early 20s and I've always wondered why. [short: What could cause a persistently high pulse rate?]

Well in all likelihood, this is cardiac autonomic neuropathy which is extremely common amongst
diabetic people. Usually there are two components to the autonomic nervous system, the sympathetic
and the parasympathetic. The sympathetic raises the heart rate. The parasympathetic slows the heart.

For whatever the reason, the parasympathetic gets injured earlier than the sympathetic. So the
system that slows the heart is impaired and so the sympathetic takes over and speeds the heart rate
up.

*And is there a way to change this?*

Well all of the neuropathies improve over time with normal blood sugars but once you have cardiac
autonomic neuropathy, you probably also have autonomic neuropathy that affects the digestive system.
So you may have gastroparesis and that makes blood sugar control much more difficult. It's this
autonomic neuropathy that enables us to do an RR interval study with an EKG machine to diagnose
gastroparesis.
